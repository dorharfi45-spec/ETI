2.2The unit circle

A circle of radius 1 centred at the origin. For an angle θ measured counter-clockwise from the positive x-axis, the terminal side meets the circle at (cos θ, sin θ). So cos θ is the x-coordinate, sin θ is the y-coordinate, and tan θ = sin θ / cos θ is undefined wherever cos θ = 0.

Quadrant signs ("All Students Take Calculus", I → II → III → IV): QI all positive; QII only sin; QIII only tan; QIV only cos.

Where tan θ sits: draw the vertical tangent line at (1, 0); extend the radius for θ until it crosses that line — the segment from (1,0) to the crossing is exactly tan θ. That's where the name comes from.

CWhen to use "and" vs "or"

Quick referencerule

Use AND when every condition must hold at the same point and the result is one intersected region: a domain restriction with a derived inequality, the double inequality from |f(x)| < k, or a case assumption (x > 0) with the inequality solved under it.

Use OR when the solution is separate alternatives, any one enough on its own: the two cases of an absolute-value equation, the two branches of |f(x)| > k, or merging the results of separate case-split branches.

        1What is a limit

        Limit of a function

        lim_(x→a) f(x) = L — as x gets close to a (with x ≠ a), f(x) gets as close to L as we want.

        Full notation (ε–δ):

        lim_(x→a) f(x) = L  ⇔  ∀ε > 0 ∃δ > 0 : 0 < |x − a| < δ ⇒ |f(x) − L| < ε

        For every band L ± ε (blue) there is a band a ± δ (green) where the graph — except at a itself — stays inside the blue band.

        One-sided limits

        • lim_(x→a⁻) f(x) — from the left (x < a)  ·  lim_(x→a⁺) f(x) — from the right (x > a)
        • lim_(x→a) f(x) = L  ⇔  lim_(x→a⁻) f(x) = lim_(x→a⁺) f(x) = L

        Left limit L₁ ≠ right limit L₂ → no limit at a. Typical at the breakpoint of a piecewise function or |·| (Functions › 2).

        Properties of a limit

        • The value at the point doesn't matter. f(a) may be anything, or not exist — the limit only looks at x near a.
        • Unique: lim_(x→a) f(x) = L₁ and lim_(x→a) f(x) = L₂ ⇒ L₁ = L₂
        • Local: f(x) = g(x) for all x ≠ a near a ⇒ lim_(x→a) f(x) = lim_(x→a) g(x)  (this is why cancelling works)

        Here lim_(x→a) f(x) = L even though f(a) ≠ L.

        Infinite limits and limits at infinity

        • lim_(x→∞) f(x) = L  ⇔  ∀ε > 0 ∃M : x > M ⇒ |f(x) − L| < ε
        • lim_(x→a) f(x) = ∞  ⇔  ∀M ∃δ > 0 : 0 < |x − a| < δ ⇒ f(x) > M

        A limit of ±∞ is not a finite limit — the arithmetic of limits needs finite ones.

        2Arithmetic of limits

        Arithmetic of limits

        If lim_(x→a) f(x) = L and lim_(x→a) g(x) = M with L, M ∈ ℝ (both exist and are finite), then:

        • lim_(x→a) [f(x) ± g(x)] = L ± M
        • lim_(x→a) c·f(x) = c·L
        • lim_(x→a) [f(x)·g(x)] = L·M
        • lim_(x→a) f(x)/g(x) = L/M  if M ≠ 0

        In words: when both limits exist (finite), you may split the limit over + − · ÷.

        When you can't use the arithmetic

        • One exists, one doesn't ⇒ the sum has no limit: lim f exists, lim g doesn't ⇒ lim (f + g) doesn't (else g = (f + g) − f would have one).
        • Neither exists ⇒ can't tell — the sum or product may still have a limit (examples next card).
        • Indeterminate forms — unknown until you rewrite the expression: 0/0, ∞/∞, ∞ − ∞, 0·∞, 1∞, 00, ∞0
        • Not indeterminate: L/∞ = 0,  ∞ + ∞ = ∞,  L·∞ = ±∞ (L ≠ 0),  L/0 (L ≠ 0) = ±∞ — check the sign from each side.

        No limits apart, a limit together

        Sum — at a = 0: f(x) = 1/x, g(x) = −1/x have no limit, but f(x) + g(x) = 0 → limit 0.

        Product — at a = 0, the sign function

        f(x) = g(x) =1x ≥ 0−1x < 0

        has a jump (no limit), but f(x)·g(x) = 1 → limit 1. Same for f(x)/g(x) = 1.

        So lim (f + g) existing does not mean lim f and lim g exist — never split a limit before you know both parts exist.

        Zero times bounded

        lim_(x→a) f(x) = 0 and ∃K > 0 : |g(x)| ≤ K near a  ⇒  lim_(x→a) f(x)·g(x) = 0

        Even when lim g doesn't exist: lim_(x→0) x·sin(1/x) = 0, since |sin(1/x)| ≤ 1.

        3Calculating limits

        When plugging in works

        f continuous at a  ⇒  lim_(x→a) f(x) = f(a)

        Every elementary function is continuous on its domain: polynomials, rational functions (denominator ≠ 0), roots, sin, cos, tan, aˣ, ln — and their sums, products, quotients and compositions (concepts 6–7).

        Plugging in gives 0/0, ∞/∞… → a is not in the domain: rewrite first.

        Polynomials and the biggest power

        • lim_(x→a) p(x) = p(a)
        • p(x) = aₙxⁿ + … + a₁x + a₀, aₙ ≠ 0:  lim_(x→±∞) p(x) = lim_(x→±∞) aₙxⁿ
        • Rational p(x)/q(x) at ±∞: deg p < deg q → 0 · equal → ratio of the leading coefficients · deg p > deg q → ±∞

        Shortened multiplication formulas

        • a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b)
        • (a ± b)² = a² ± 2ab + b²
        • a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²)
        • a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²)

        Read right-to-left they factor; left-to-right they build the "partner" that cancels a root.

        Multiplying by 1

        Multiplying by A/A (= 1, for A ≠ 0) changes the form, not the value:

        • (1/xⁿ)/(1/xⁿ) — divide top and bottom by the biggest power (x → ∞)
        • (√x + 2)/(√x + 2) — the conjugate: (√x − 2)(√x + 2) = x − 4 removes the root

        4L'Hôpital's rule

        L'Hôpital's rule

        If f, g are differentiable near a (except maybe at a), g'(x) ≠ 0 near a,

        lim_(x→a) f(x) = lim_(x→a) g(x) = 0  (or both ±∞),  and lim_(x→a) f'(x)/g'(x) = L (L finite or ±∞)

        ⇒  lim_(x→a) f(x)/g(x) = lim_(x→a) f'(x)/g'(x) = L

        Also for x → ±∞ and one-sided limits. Only for 0/0 or ∞/∞. Differentiate top and bottom separately — not the quotient rule. Derivatives: Precalc › 1 Derivatives, 3 Trig calculus, 4 Exp & log calculus.

        Other forms into 0/0 or ∞/∞

        • 0·∞:  f·g = f/(1/g)
        • ∞ − ∞:  common denominator, or the conjugate
        • 1∞, 00, ∞0:  fg = eg·ln f → find lim g·ln f

        5Continuity

        Continuity at a point

        f is continuous at a  ⇔  lim_(x→a) f(x) = f(a)

        Full notation: ∀ε > 0 ∃δ > 0 : |x − a| < δ ⇒ |f(x) − f(a)| < ε

        Three conditions: f(a) exists · lim_(x→a) f(x) exists · they are equal. One fails → not continuous:

        One-sided continuity and intervals

        • From the right: lim_(x→a⁺) f(x) = f(a)  ·  from the left: lim_(x→a⁻) f(x) = f(a)
        • On (a, b): continuous at every point.
        • On [a, b]: on (a, b) + from the right at a + from the left at b.

        6Arithmetic of continuity

        Arithmetic of continuous functions

        f, g continuous at a  ⇒  f ± g, c·f, f·g continuous at a, and f/g too if g(a) ≠ 0.

        x² + sin x is continuous everywhere; (x + 1)/(x − 2) for every x ≠ 2.

        Continuous and not continuous

        • f continuous at a, g not  ⇒  f ± g not continuous at a
        • … and if also f(a) ≠ 0  ⇒  f·g, g/f not continuous at a
        • Both not continuous ⇒ can't tell.

        x + sgn x is not continuous at 0; but x·sgn x = |x| is (there f(0) = 0). Proved by contradiction — see the method.

        7Composition and continuity

        Composition of continuous functions

        g continuous at a and f continuous at g(a)  ⇒  f∘g continuous at a

        sin(x²), √(x² + 1), ecos x — continuous wherever defined. Composition itself: Functions › 4.

        Limit of a composition

        • lim_(x→a) g(x) = b and f continuous at b  ⇒  lim_(x→a) f(g(x)) = f(lim_(x→a) g(x)) = f(b)
        • Substitution: lim_(x→a) g(x) = b, g(x) ≠ b near a, lim_(t→b) f(t) = L  ⇒  lim_(x→a) f(g(x)) = L

        The limit goes inside a continuous function: lim_(x→0) cos(x² + π) = cos π = −1. The second line is why a help variable t works.

        8Intermediate value theorem

        Intermediate value theorem

        f continuous on [a, b] and y between f(a) and f(b)  ⇒  ∃c ∈ [a, b] : f(c) = y

        A continuous graph can't get from f(a) to f(b) without crossing every level in between.

        A root between a sign change

        f continuous on [a, b] and f(a)·f(b) < 0  ⇒  ∃c ∈ (a, b) : f(c) = 0

        Says a root exists — not where, not how many. Continuity on the whole [a, b] is a must: 1/x on [−1, 1] changes sign with no root.

        1What is a derivative

        Derivative at a point

        f'(a) = lim_(h→0) (f(a + h) − f(a))/h

        Same limit, written with x = a + h:

        f'(a) = lim_(x→a) (f(x) − f(a))/(x − a)

        If this limit exists and is finite, f is differentiable at a and the limit is the derivative. The derivative function f' gives this number at every x where it exists. Other notation: y', df/dx.

        The quotient is the slope of the secant (green) through two points of the graph. As h → 0 the second point slides to the first, and the secant turns into the tangent (dashed). Try it live in Precalc › Derivatives › Secant → tangent.

        What the derivative tells you

        • Slope: f'(a) is the slope of the tangent to the graph at a.
        • Tangent line: y = f(a) + f'(a)·(x − a)
        • Rate of change: how fast f changes at a. Near a: f(a + h) ≈ f(a) + f'(a)·h
        • Direction: f' > 0 on an interval → rising; f' < 0 → falling; f'(a) = 0 → horizontal tangent (Derivatives › 7).

        From the definition: for f(x) = x², (f(x + h) − f(x))/h = (2xh + h²)/h = 2x + h → 2x. So f'(x) = 2x: the slope at each point of the parabola.

        2Differentiation formulas

        Powers, polynomials and roots

        • (c)' = 0  ·  (c·f)' = c·f'  ·  (f + g)' = f' + g'
        • (xⁿ)' = n·xⁿ⁻¹
        • (√x)' = 1/(2√x)  (x > 0)
        • (ⁿ√x)' = 1/(n·ⁿ√(xⁿ⁻¹))
        • Any real power, x > 0: (xʳ)' = r·xʳ⁻¹. A root or a fraction is a power: 1/x² = x⁻², so (1/x²)' = −2x⁻³ = −2/x³
        • Polynomial, term by term: (4x³ − 5x + 7)' = 12x² − 5

        The slope grows with x: the derivative of x² is the straight line 2x. Differentiating lowers a power by one.

        Why, from the definition

        • Powers: by the binomial formula (x + h)ⁿ = xⁿ + n·xⁿ⁻¹·h + h²·(…), so
        • ((x + h)ⁿ − xⁿ)/h = n·xⁿ⁻¹ + h·(…) → n·xⁿ⁻¹
        • Root (multiply by the conjugate, Limits › 3):
        • With s = √(x + h) + √x: (√(x + h) − √x)/h = ((x + h) − x)/(h·s) = 1/s
        • As h → 0, s → 2√x, so the limit is 1/(2√x).
        • Sum and constant: split the quotient and use the arithmetic of limits.
        • Real power: xʳ = er·ln x, so by the chain rule (xʳ)' = xʳ·(r/x) = r·xʳ⁻¹.

        Product and quotient

        • Product: (f·g)' = f'·g + f·g'
        • Quotient: (f/g)' = (f'·g − f·g')/g²  (g ≠ 0)
        • Reciprocal: (1/g)' = −g'/g²

        Example: (x²·sin x)' = 2x·sin x + x²·cos x

        Think of f·g as an area. When both sides grow, the area gains two strips, Δf·g and f·Δg. The small corner Δf·Δg disappears in the limit. That is the two terms of the product rule.

        Why, from the definition

        • Product: add and subtract f(x + h)·g(x) in the numerator:
        • f(x + h)g(x + h) − f(x)g(x)
        • = f(x + h)·(g(x + h) − g(x)) + (f(x + h) − f(x))·g(x)
        • Divide by h and let h → 0. Since f is differentiable it is continuous, so f(x + h) → f(x), and the result is f·g' + f'·g.
        • Reciprocal: (1/g(x + h) − 1/g(x))/h = −(g(x + h) − g(x))/h · 1/(g(x + h)·g(x)) → −g'/g²
        • Quotient: f/g = f·(1/g), so (f/g)' = f'/g − f·g'/g² = (f'·g − f·g')/g²

        Composite functions (chain rule)

        (f(g(x)))' = f'(g(x))·g'(x)

        The derivative of the outer function, at the inner function, times the derivative of the inner function.

        • ((3x² + 1)⁵)' = 5(3x² + 1)⁴·6x
        • (√(x² + 1))' = 2x/(2√(x² + 1)) = x/√(x² + 1)
        • (sin(x³))' = cos(x³)·3x²

        A small change in x is multiplied by g' on the way through g, and that result is multiplied by f' on the way through f. Rates multiply.

        Why, from the definition

        • Write t = g(x + h) and b = g(x). Multiply by 1, as long as t ≠ b:
        • (f(t) − f(b))/h = (f(t) − f(b))/(t − b) · (t − b)/h
        • t → b because g is continuous. The first quotient → f'(g(x)), the second → g'(x).
        • The case g(x + h) = g(x) near x needs a small extra argument in a full proof; the formula stays the same.

        Trigonometric functions

        • (sin x)' = cos x
        • (cos x)' = −sin x
        • (tan x)' = 1/cos²x = 1 + tan²x
        • With an inner function g: (sin g)' = cos g·g'  ·  (cos g)' = −sin g·g'  ·  (tan g)' = g'/cos²g

        The green curve is the slope of the blue one. At the top of sin x the tangent is flat, and cos x = 0 there.

        Why, from the definition

        • Two base limits: lim_(h→0) (sin h)/h = 1 and lim_(h→0) (cos h − 1)/h = 0
        • Sine: use sin(x + h) = sin x·cos h + cos x·sin h (Trig base › Identities):
        • (sin(x + h) − sin x)/h = sin x·(cos h − 1)/h + cos x·(sin h)/h → cos x
        • Cosine: use cos(x + h) = cos x·cos h − sin x·sin h:
        • (cos(x + h) − cos x)/h = cos x·(cos h − 1)/h − sin x·(sin h)/h → −sin x
        • Tangent: the quotient rule on sin x / cos x gives (cos²x + sin²x)/cos²x = 1/cos²x

        Exponential and logarithmic functions

        • (eˣ)' = eˣ
        • (aˣ)' = aˣ·ln a  (a > 0)
        • (ln x)' = 1/x  (x > 0)
        • (log_a x)' = 1/(x·ln a)
        • With an inner function f: (ef)' = ef·f'  ·  (af)' = af·ln a·f'  ·  (ln f)' = f'/f

        eˣ and ln x mirror each other in y = x. eˣ has slope 1 at x = 0, and ln x has slope 1 at x = 1.

        Why, from the definition

        • eˣ: (eˣ⁺ʰ − eˣ)/h = eˣ·(eʰ − 1)/h → eˣ, because lim_(h→0) (eʰ − 1)/h = 1. That is what makes e special: the tangent at 0 has slope 1 (Precalc › Exp & log calculus).
        • aˣ: aˣ = ex·ln a, so the chain rule gives aˣ·ln a.
        • ln x: (ln(x + h) − ln x)/h = ln(1 + h/x)/h. With t = h/x this is (1/x)·ln(1 + t)/t → 1/x, because lim_(t→0) ln(1 + t)/t = 1.
        • log_a x: log_a x = ln x / ln a, a constant times ln x.
        • ln f: the chain rule with outer ln and inner f.

        3Differentiability

        Differentiable at a point

        f differentiable at a  ⇔  lim_(h→0) (f(a + h) − f(a))/h exists and is finite.

        One-sided derivatives:

        • f'₋(a) = lim_(h→0⁻) (f(a + h) − f(a))/h
        • f'₊(a) = lim_(h→0⁺) (f(a + h) − f(a))/h
        • f differentiable at a  ⇔  f'₋(a) = f'₊(a), both finite

        Differentiable on (a, b): at every point. On [a, b]: also one-sided at the ends.

        Differentiable implies continuous

        f differentiable at a  ⇒  f continuous at a

        The other way is false: f(x) = |x| is continuous at 0 and not differentiable there.

        Proof: f(x) − f(a) = (f(x) − f(a))/(x − a) · (x − a) → f'(a)·0 = 0, so f(x) → f(a).

        Use it backwards: not continuous at a ⇒ not differentiable at a. No calculation needed.

        Where a function is not differentiable

        Four ways the limit of the quotient can fail:

        • Not continuous: a jump or a hole. Example: f(x) jumps at a.
        • Corner: f'₋(a) ≠ f'₊(a). Example: |x| at 0.
        • Vertical tangent or cusp: the quotient → ±∞. Examples: ∛x and ∛(x²) at 0.
        • Oscillation: the quotient has no limit. Example: x·sin(1/x) at 0, with f(0) = 0.

        Also, of course: a point outside the domain.

        Limit of the derivative

        f continuous at a, differentiable for x ≠ a near a, and lim_(x→a) f'(x) = L:

        • L finite  ⇒  f differentiable at a and f'(a) = L
        • L = ±∞  ⇒  f not differentiable at a
        • One-sided too: lim_(x→a⁻) f'(x) = f'₋(a) and lim_(x→a⁺) f'(x) = f'₊(a)

        Consequence: if f'(a) exists and lim_(x→a) f'(x) exists, then the two are equal, so f' is continuous at a. A derivative function can't have a hole or a jump.

        Why: by the mean value theorem (Derivatives › 7), (f(x) − f(a))/(x − a) = f'(c) for some c between a and x. As x → a, c → a, so the quotient has the same limit as f'.

        Continuity of f at a is required. If lim_(x→a) f'(x) does not exist, the theorem says nothing: go to the definition. Example: x²·sin(1/x), f(0) = 0, has f'(0) = 0, yet lim_(x→0) f'(x) does not exist.

        When the derivative formula is undefined at a point

        If the formula for f' has a denominator that is 0 at a, or is undefined at a in any other way, treat it as a warning sign. It is not yet a verdict: the formula only holds where the rules were allowed.

        • Usually not differentiable: f = ∛x, f' = 1/(3∛(x²)) → +∞ at 0. Not differentiable.
        • But not always: f = √(x⁴), f' = 4x³/(2√(x⁴)) is undefined at 0, yet f = x² is differentiable there.

        Decide with the limit of the derivative (if f is continuous at a), or with the definition.

        Method: Is a function differentiable at a point?

        4Arithmetic of differentiability

        Arithmetic of differentiable functions

        f, g differentiable at a  ⇒  these are differentiable at a:

        • f ± g, with (f ± g)'(a) = f'(a) ± g'(a)
        • c·f, with (c·f)'(a) = c·f'(a)
        • f·g, with (f·g)'(a) = f'(a)·g(a) + f(a)·g'(a)
        • f/g, if g(a) ≠ 0, with (f/g)'(a) = (f'(a)·g(a) − f(a)·g'(a))/g(a)²

        Composition: g differentiable at a and f differentiable at g(a)  ⇒  f∘g differentiable at a, with (f∘g)'(a) = f'(g(a))·g'(a)

        Differentiable on their whole domain: polynomials, sin, cos, tan, eˣ, aˣ, ln. Roots: on x > 0, not at 0. |x|: everywhere except 0.

        When you can't use the arithmetic

        The theorem only works in one direction: all parts differentiable ⇒ the result is differentiable. If one part is not differentiable at a, the theorem says nothing. Go to the definition.

        • Sum: f differentiable, g not ⇒ f + g not. If it were, g = (f + g) − f would be differentiable.
        • Both not differentiable, sum is: |x| + (−|x|) = 0
        • Product is differentiable: x·|x| at 0, and |x|·|x| = x²
        • Product is not: 1·|x| at 0
        • Composition: |x|² is differentiable at 0, |sin x| and sin|x| are not.

        The factor x flattens the corner: x·|x| has tangent slope 0 from both sides of 0.

        5Local extrema

        Local extremum point

        • Local maximum at a: ∃δ > 0 : |x − a| < δ ⇒ f(x) ≤ f(a)
        • Local minimum at a: ∃δ > 0 : |x − a| < δ ⇒ f(x) ≥ f(a)

        "Local" means compared only with nearby points. A local maximum can be lower than a local minimum somewhere else.

        Fermat's theorem

        f has a local extremum at an interior point a and f is differentiable at a  ⇒  f'(a) = 0

        The other way is false: f(x) = x³ has f'(0) = 0 and no extremum at 0.

        Proof (maximum): near a, f(x) − f(a) ≤ 0. So the quotient (f(x) − f(a))/(x − a) is ≥ 0 from the left and ≤ 0 from the right. Both one-sided limits equal f'(a), so f'(a) = 0.

        Critical points and the first derivative test

        A critical point is an interior point of the domain where f'(a) = 0 or f'(a) does not exist. Every local extremum is at a critical point, but not every critical point is an extremum.

        First derivative test (f continuous at a):

        • f' changes from + to − at a ⇒ local maximum
        • f' changes from − to + at a ⇒ local minimum
        • No sign change ⇒ no extremum

        The test works because the sign of f' tells where f rises and falls (Derivatives › 7, Monotonicity).

        6Absolute extrema

        Absolute extremum point

        • Absolute maximum of f on D at a ∈ D: ∀x ∈ D : f(x) ≤ f(a)
        • Absolute minimum of f on D at a ∈ D: ∀x ∈ D : f(x) ≥ f(a)
        • The value is unique on a given domain: one largest value, one smallest value.
        • The point need not be: sin x reaches its maximum 1 at infinitely many points.
        • It may not exist.
        • Where to look: a critical point inside the domain, or an end of the domain.

        Extreme value theorem

        f continuous on a closed interval [a, b]  ⇒  ∃c, d ∈ [a, b] : ∀x ∈ [a, b] : f(c) ≤ f(x) ≤ f(d)

        A continuous function on a closed interval reaches an absolute minimum and an absolute maximum.

        Both conditions are needed. Functions with no absolute extremum:

        • Open interval: f(x) = x on (0, 1) has neither.
        • Open at one end: f(x) = 1/x on (0, 1] has no maximum.
        • Not bounded domain: f(x) = eˣ on ℝ has neither.
        • Not continuous: f(x) = x on [0, 1), f(1) = 0, has no maximum.

        Functions that go to infinity at both ends

        f continuous on (a, b) (a, b may be ±∞):

        • lim_(x→a⁺) f(x) = lim_(x→b⁻) f(x) = +∞  ⇒  absolute minimum, no absolute maximum
        • lim_(x→a⁺) f(x) = lim_(x→b⁻) f(x) = −∞  ⇒  absolute maximum, no absolute minimum

        Why: take any point x₀. Near the ends f > f(x₀), so outside some closed interval [c, d] containing x₀ the function is above f(x₀). On [c, d] the extreme value theorem gives a minimum, and it is the minimum everywhere.

        Same idea with a finite limit: if both end limits are finite and some f(x₀) is below both, there is an absolute minimum (above both: an absolute maximum).

        7Rolle's theorem and the mean value theorem

        Rolle's theorem

        If:

        1. f is continuous on [a, b]
        2. f is differentiable on (a, b)
        3. f(a) = f(b)

        then ∃c ∈ (a, b) : f'(c) = 0

        A graph that leaves a height and comes back must turn around somewhere, and at the turn the tangent is horizontal.

        Proof: by the extreme value theorem, f reaches a maximum and a minimum on [a, b]. If both are at the ends, f is constant and any c works. Otherwise one is at an interior point c, and Fermat's theorem gives f'(c) = 0.

        When a condition of Rolle's theorem fails

        Drop any one condition and the conclusion can fail:

        • Not continuous at an end: f(x) = x on [0, 1), f(1) = 0. Then f(0) = f(1), but f' = 1 everywhere inside.
        • Not differentiable inside: f(x) = |x| on [−1, 1]. Then f(−1) = f(1), but f' = ±1.
        • Different end values: f(x) = x on [0, 1]. Then f' = 1 everywhere.

        The conditions are sufficient, not necessary: when one fails, a point with f' = 0 may still exist. The theorem just doesn't promise it.

        Mean value theorem

        f continuous on [a, b] and differentiable on (a, b)  ⇒  ∃c ∈ (a, b) : f'(c) = (f(b) − f(a))/(b − a)

        Somewhere the tangent is parallel to the chord: the instant rate equals the average rate. Rolle's theorem is the case f(a) = f(b).

        Proof: apply Rolle's theorem to h(x) = f(x) − (f(b) − f(a))/(b − a)·(x − a), which has h(a) = h(b) = f(a).

        Monotonicity

        f continuous on an interval I and differentiable inside it:

        • f' > 0 inside I ⇒ f strictly increasing on I
        • f' < 0 inside I ⇒ f strictly decreasing on I
        • f' = 0 inside I ⇒ f constant on I
        • f' = g' inside I ⇒ f = g + c on I
        • Comparing: f(a) ≥ g(a) and f' > g' for x > a ⇒ f(x) > g(x) for x > a

        Why: for x₁ < x₂ the mean value theorem gives f(x₂) − f(x₁) = f'(c)·(x₂ − x₁). The sign of f'(c) decides.

        It must be an interval. 1/x has f' < 0 on its domain but is not decreasing there. The converse is weaker: x³ is strictly increasing with f'(0) = 0. A f' that is 0 only at single points doesn't spoil strict increase.

        Roots of f and roots of f'

        • Between two roots of f there is a root of f' (Rolle).
        • f has n roots ⇒ f' has at least n − 1 roots
        • f' has k roots ⇒ f has at most k + 1 roots
        • f' has no roots ⇒ f has at most one root
        • A polynomial of degree n has at most n real roots; if n is odd, at least one.

        These count at most. To show a root really exists, use the intermediate value theorem (Limits › 8). All of this assumes f is differentiable on the whole interval.

        8Convexity and concavity

        Convex and concave

        The second derivative is the derivative of the derivative: f'' = (f')'.

        f differentiable on an interval I:

        • Convex on I: f' increasing. The graph bends upward, lies above its tangents and below its chords.
        • Concave on I: f' decreasing. The graph bends downward, lies below its tangents and above its chords.

        Test: f'' > 0 on I ⇒ convex  ·  f'' < 0 on I ⇒ concave

        On a convex stretch the slope keeps growing; on a concave stretch it keeps shrinking. That is why the sign of f'', the rate of change of the slope, decides.

        Inflection point

        a is an inflection point of f if f is continuous at a, has a tangent there, and changes from convex to concave (or the other way) at a.

        • Candidates: f''(a) = 0 or f''(a) undefined, with a in the domain.
        • f''(a) = 0 is not enough: x⁴ has f''(0) = 0 and is convex on both sides. The sign of f'' must change.
        • f'' may be undefined at an inflection point: ∛x at 0, with a vertical tangent.
        • A sign change at a point outside the domain is not an inflection point: 1/x at 0.

        At an inflection point the graph crosses its own tangent.

        Second derivative test

        f'(a) = 0 and f''(a) exists:

        • f''(a) > 0 ⇒ local minimum
        • f''(a) < 0 ⇒ local maximum
        • f''(a) = 0 ⇒ no conclusion: x⁴ (minimum), −x⁴ (maximum), x³ (neither)

        A horizontal tangent on a convex stretch can only be a bottom, on a concave stretch only a top.

        Inequalities from convexity

        • Convex on I, a ∈ I: f(x) ≥ f(a) + f'(a)·(x − a) for all x ∈ I
        • Concave on I, a ∈ I: f(x) ≤ f(a) + f'(a)·(x − a) for all x ∈ I
        • Convex on [a, b]: below the chord between a and b. Concave: above it.

        The right side is the tangent line at a. If one side of an inequality is a line, it is often a tangent or a chord of the other side.

        1What an integral is

        Integrals and antiderivatives

        F is an antiderivative of f when F'(x) = f(x). Then

        ∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + C

        Integration is differentiation run backwards: F' = f  ⇔  ∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + C

        In words: differentiating asks "how fast is this function changing?". Integrating asks the reverse: "which function changes like this?". If you know the speed at every moment, the integral gives you back the position.

        Why the + C: a constant has derivative 0, so x², x² + 1 and x² − 7 all have derivative 2x. The derivative can't tell them apart, so the integral gives the whole family: ∫ 2x dx = x² + C, where C is any number.

        Check any answer by differentiating it: you must get the function under the integral back.

        Every antiderivative is the same curve moved up or down. Moving a curve up doesn't change its slope, so all of them have the same derivative.

        The integral sign and its bounds

        • Indefinite integral: ∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + C. No bounds. The answer is a family of functions.
        • Definite integral: ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx. The answer is one number.

        Reading the symbol: ∫ is a stretched S, for "sum". f(x) is the integrand, the function being integrated. a is the lower bound and b the upper bound: where the sum starts and ends on the x-axis. dx says that x is the variable.

        What the number means: the definite integral is the signed area between the graph and the x-axis from a to b. Area above the axis counts plus, area below counts minus (Integrals › 7).

        The two kinds are tied together by Newton–Leibniz (Integrals › 2): find F from the indefinite integral, then ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a).

        What dx means

        dx names the variable you integrate by, and stands for a very thin width along the x-axis.

        The picture: cut the area under the graph into very thin vertical slices. One slice at x is almost a rectangle: height f(x), width dx, area f(x) dx. The integral adds up all these slices from a to b. That is why f(x) and dx are written next to each other, like a multiplication.

        Which variable: in ∫ x·t dt the variable is t, so x is treated as a constant. Change the letter after d and you change the question.

        How thin is "very thin"? Darboux and Riemann sums (next card) make it exact: the slices get thinner and thinner, and the integral is what the sum tends to.

        Darboux and Riemann sums

        Split [a, b] into n strips of width Δx = (b − a)/(n), with points x_(0) = a < x_(1) < … < x_(n) = b.

        • Lower sum L: each strip's rectangle is as high as the lowest value of f on it.
        • Upper sum U: each rectangle is as high as the highest value of f on it.
        • Riemann sum: any point c_(i) in each strip: ∑_(i=1)^(n) f(c_(i))·Δx. It always lies between L and U.
        • f is integrable on [a, b] when L and U come together as the strips get thinner. Then ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx = lim_(n→∞) ∑_(i=1)^(n) f(c_(i))·Δx
        • Theorem: every function continuous on [a, b] is integrable on it.

        In words: we can't measure a curved area directly, but we can measure rectangles. The lower sum is too small, the upper sum is too big, and the true area is squeezed between them. Make the strips thinner and the two estimates close in on the same number: that number is the integral.

        For f(x) = x² on [0, 1]: the green rectangles are the lower sum, the outlined ones the upper sum. With 4, 8, 16 strips the gap between them shrinks towards the exact area 1/3.

        2The fundamental theorem and Newton–Leibniz

        Fundamental theorem of calculus

        f continuous on [a, b] and F(x) = ∫_(a)^(x) f(t) dt  ⇒  F'(x) = f(x) for every x ∈ (a, b)

        The area function is an antiderivative of the function under it. This is the theorem that links integrals and derivatives.

        What F is: the area under f from the fixed start a up to a moving end x. Move x and the area changes, so F is a function of x. The letter inside is t only because x is already taken by the end.

        Why it is true: move the end from x to x + h. The area grows by a thin strip of width h and height about f(x), so F(x + h) − F(x) ≈ f(x)·h. Divide by h: (F(x + h) − F(x))/h ≈ f(x). As h → 0 the strip becomes an exact rectangle, and the left side is the derivative F'(x) (Derivatives › 1). The area grows at the rate f(x).

        The shaded area is F(x). The thin outlined strip is what moving to x + h adds: about f(x)·h.

        Newton–Leibniz theorem

        f continuous on [a, b] and F any antiderivative of f  ⇒  ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)

        Written: ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx = [F(x)]_(a)^(b) = F(b) − F(a)

        Where it comes from: by the fundamental theorem, the area function A(x) = ∫_(a)^(x) f(t) dt is an antiderivative of f. Any other antiderivative differs from it by a constant: F = A + C (Derivatives › 7, Monotonicity). So F(b) − F(a) = A(b) − A(a) = A(b) − 0, which is the whole area.

        In words: the change in a function between two points equals the area under its derivative over that interval: ∫_(a)^(b) f'(x) dx = f(b) − f(a). If f' is a speed, the area under it is the distance covered.

        Signed area: the result is the signed area. It equals the geometric area only where the function under the integral is ≥ 0.

        The + C doesn't matter here: (F(b) + C) − (F(a) + C) = F(b) − F(a), so any antiderivative gives the same answer.

        Left: the area under f'(x) = x from 1 to 3 is 4. Right: f(x) = x²/2 rises from 1/2 to 9/2, also by 4.

        3Linear properties

        Sums and constants

        • ∫ (f(x) + g(x)) dx = ∫ f(x) dx + ∫ g(x) dx
        • ∫ k·f(x) dx = k·∫ f(x) dx  (k a constant)
        • The same with bounds: ∫_(a)^(b) (α·f(x) + β·g(x)) dx = α∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx + β∫_(a)^(b) g(x) dx

        Why: the derivative has the same rules: (F + G)' = F' + G' and (k·F)' = k·F'. Run them backwards and you get these.

        Use: split a long integrand into simple pieces and integrate each one: ∫ (3x² − 4x + 5) dx = 3·∫ x² dx − 4·∫ x dx + ∫ 5 dx = x³ − 2x² + 5x + C

        There is no such rule for products or quotients: ∫ f·g dx is not ∫ f dx·∫ g dx. Products need substitution or parts (Integrals › 5, 6).

        Properties of the definite integral

        • Same bounds: ∫_(a)^(a) f(x) dx = 0
        • Swapping the bounds flips the sign: ∫_(b)^(a) f(x) dx = −∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx
        • Splitting the interval: ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx = ∫_(a)^(c) f(x) dx + ∫_(c)^(b) f(x) dx

        In words: no width, no area. Walking the interval backwards counts every slice with the opposite sign. And an area can be cut into two pieces at any point c and added back together.

        Splitting is what lets you integrate a piecewise function or an absolute value: cut at each breakpoint (Integrals › 7).

        4Basic integration techniques

        Polynomials and roots

        • Power rule: ∫ xⁿ dx = xⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1) + C  (n ≠ −1)
        • The missing case: ∫ 1/(x) dx = ln|x| + C
        • Constants: ∫ k dx = k·x + C
        • Roots are powers: √x = x^(1/2), ∛x = x^(1/3), 1/√x = x^(−1/2)

        Why: differentiating lowers the power by one and multiplies by it. Integrating does the opposite: raise the power by one, then divide by the new power. Check: (xⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1))' = xⁿ.

        Why n ≠ −1: for n = −1 the rule would divide by 0. That case is ln|x|. The absolute value is there so it also works for x < 0, where ln x is not defined but (ln|x|)' = 1/x still holds.

        Examples: ∫ √x dx = ∫ x^(1/2) dx = (x^(3/2))/(3/2) + C = (2/3)·x√x + C  ·  ∫ 1/(x³) dx = ∫ x⁻³ dx = (x⁻²)/(−2) + C = −1/(2x²) + C

        A linear function to a power

        • ∫ (ax + b)ⁿ dx = ((ax + b)ⁿ⁺¹)/(a(n + 1)) + C  (n ≠ −1, a ≠ 0)
        • ∫ 1/(ax + b) dx = (1/a)·ln|ax + b| + C

        Why the extra 1/a: differentiating (ax + b)ⁿ⁺¹ gives (n + 1)(ax + b)ⁿ·a. The inside's derivative a appears (chain rule, Derivatives › 2), so we divide by it in advance.

        Example: ∫ (3x − 1)⁴ dx = ((3x − 1)⁵)/(3·5) + C = ((3x − 1)⁵)/(15) + C

        This works only when the inside is linear. For (x² + 1)⁴ the inside's derivative 2x is not a constant: expand it, or use substitution if x is also there (Integrals › 5).

        Integrals that give ln

        ∫ f'(x)/(f(x)) dx = ln|f(x)| + C

        Why: by the chain rule, (ln|f(x)|)' = f'(x)/f(x) (Derivatives › 2).

        How to spot it: the numerator is the derivative of the denominator, maybe up to a constant.

        Examples: ∫ 2x/(x² + 1) dx = ln(x² + 1) + C (no |·| needed: x² + 1 > 0)  ·  ∫ cos x/sin x dx = ln|sin x| + C

        Almost the right numerator? Fix it first: the method "Bring a quotient to the form f′/f".

        Sine and cosine

        • ∫ sin x dx = −cos x + C
        • ∫ cos x dx = sin x + C
        • ∫ sin(ax + b) dx = −(1/a)·cos(ax + b) + C
        • ∫ cos(ax + b) dx = (1/a)·sin(ax + b) + C

        Why: (cos x)' = −sin x, so (−cos x)' = sin x, and (sin x)' = cos x (Derivatives › 2). Watch the minus sign: it belongs to the integral of sin.

        Example: ∫ cos(2x + 1) dx = (1/2)·sin(2x + 1) + C. Check: ((1/2)·sin(2x + 1))' = (1/2)·cos(2x + 1)·2 = cos(2x + 1)

        When the inside is not linear, like sin(x²), these rules don't work. Substitution does, when the inside's derivative is also there: ∫ x·sin(x²) dx (Integrals › 5).

        Exponential functions

        • ∫ eˣ dx = eˣ + C
        • ∫ e^(ax + b) dx = (1/a)·e^(ax + b) + C  (a ≠ 0)

        Why: eˣ is its own derivative (Derivatives › 2). With a linear inside, the chain rule brings out an a, so we divide by it.

        Example: ∫ e^(−2x) dx = −(1/2)·e^(−2x) + C

        5Substitution

        What the substitution does

        ∫ f(g(x))·g'(x) dx = ∫ f(t) dt, with t = g(x) and dt = g'(x) dx

        With bounds: ∫_(a)^(b) f(g(x))·g'(x) dx = ∫_(g(a))^(g(b)) f(t) dt

        Where it comes from: it is the chain rule run backwards. If F' = f, the chain rule gives (F(g(x)))' = f(g(x))·g'(x). So the integral of the right side is F(g(x)) + C = F(t) + C, which is exactly ∫ f(t) dt.

        What dt = g'(x) dx means: a thin slice of width dx on the x-axis becomes a slice of width dt = g'(x)·dx on the t-axis. Where g changes fast, the slices are stretched, and the integrand is rescaled to match, so the area stays the same.

        How to spot it: a function inside another function (the inside is g(x)), and the inside's derivative g'(x) is also multiplying, maybe up to a constant.

        ∫_(0)^(1) 2x·e^(x²) dx with t = x² becomes ∫_(0)^(1) eᵗ dt. The graphs look different, but the areas are the same: e − 1 ≈ 1.72.

        6Integration by parts

        Integration by parts formula

        ∫ u·v' dx = u·v − ∫ u'·v dx

        With bounds: ∫_(a)^(b) u·v' dx = [u·v]_(a)^(b) − ∫_(a)^(b) u'·v dx

        Where it comes from, step by step:

        1. Product rule (Derivatives › 2): (u·v)' = u'·v + u·v'
        2. Integrate both sides: u·v = ∫ u'·v dx + ∫ u·v' dx. The left side is an antiderivative of its own derivative.
        3. Move one integral across: ∫ u·v' dx = u·v − ∫ u'·v dx

        What it buys you: it swaps one integral for another. It helps when the new one, ∫ u'·v dx, is easier: you choose u as the factor that gets simpler when you differentiate it.

        The big rectangle minus the small one is u₂v₂ − u₁v₁. The curve splits that L-shaped region into the area under it, ∫ v du, and the area beside it, ∫ u dv. So ∫ u dv = [uv] − ∫ v du.

        7Definite integrals and area

        Integral value and area

        • Value of the integral: ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx. Parts below the x-axis count negative.
        • Area between the graph and the x-axis: ∫_(a)^(b) |f(x)| dx. Everything counts positive.

        In words: the integral adds heights times widths, and a height below the axis is a negative number. So a piece below the axis subtracts. The area is a geometric size, never negative: you flip the negative pieces before adding.

        When they are equal: only when f ≥ 0 on the whole interval.

        For sin x on [0, 2π] the two humps cancel in the integral but add up in the area. Try other functions live in Precalc › Integrals.

        Area between two curves

        Where f(x) ≥ g(x) on [a, b]:  area = ∫_(a)^(b) (f(x) − g(x)) dx

        In words: each thin slice now goes from the lower curve up to the upper curve, so its height is top − bottom. This works anywhere, even below the x-axis, because the difference is still the slice's height.

        When the curves cross inside the interval, the top one changes. Split at the crossing points and use top − bottom on each piece.

        y = x and y = x³ cross at −1, 0 and 1. On (0, 1) the line is on top, on (−1, 0) the cubic is.

        Even and odd functions on a symmetric interval

        • Even (f(−x) = f(x), mirror image in the y-axis): ∫_(−a)^(a) f(x) dx = 2∫_(0)^(a) f(x) dx
        • Odd (f(−x) = −f(x), symmetric about the origin): ∫_(−a)^(a) f(x) dx = 0, but the area is 2∫_(0)^(a) |f(x)| dx

        Examples: x², cos x, |x| are even. x³, sin x, x are odd.

        Why: an even graph has two equal halves, so do one and double it. An odd graph's left half is the right half flipped below the axis, so their signed values cancel. The areas don't cancel: both halves have the same size.

        8Estimating integrals

        Comparison of integrals

        f(x) ≤ g(x) for all x ∈ [a, b] (a < b)  ⇒  ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx ≤ ∫_(a)^(b) g(x) dx

        In words: if one graph is never above the other, the area under it can't be bigger. Each thin slice of f is no taller than the matching slice of g.

        Use it to estimate an integral you can't calculate: put it between two simpler functions whose integrals you can calculate.

        Bounds from the minimum and maximum

        f continuous on [a, b], with minimum m and maximum M there  ⇒  m(b − a) ≤ ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx ≤ M(b − a)

        Why: compare f with the two constant functions m ≤ f(x) ≤ M. A constant's integral is a rectangle: height times width.

        Finding m and M: check the two ends and the critical points inside (Derivatives › 6). Only when f is monotone on the interval are they simply f(a) and f(b).

        The integral is the area under the curve. It is more than the low rectangle (green) and less than the high one (blue). Here the maximum is inside the interval, not at an end.

        9Improper integrals

        What an improper integral is

        • ∫_(a)^(∞) f(x) dx = lim_(r→∞) ∫_(a)^(r) f(x) dx
        • ∫_(−∞)^(b) f(x) dx = lim_(r→−∞) ∫_(r)^(b) f(x) dx
        • Both bounds infinite: split at any point c: ∫_(−∞)^(∞) f(x) dx = ∫_(−∞)^(c) f(x) dx + ∫_(c)^(∞) f(x) dx. Both pieces must converge.

        It converges if the limit is a finite number, and diverges otherwise (infinite, or no limit).

        In words: we can't plug in ∞, so we stop at a finite end r, calculate as usual, and then let r run off to infinity (limits at infinity: Limits › 1). An area that goes on forever can still add up to a finite number, if the graph falls towards 0 fast enough.

        ∫_(1)^(r) 1/(x²) dx = 1 − 1/r → 1: the endless area under 1/x² from 1 on is exactly 1.

        Improper integrals of unbounded functions

        If f blows up at the end a (continuous on (a, b]):

        ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx = lim_(r→a⁺) ∫_(r)^(b) f(x) dx

        The same at the end b with r → b⁻. A blow-up inside the interval: split there.

        In words: the bound is finite, but the graph shoots up to infinity at it, so we can't plug it in. Stop just before it at r, calculate, and let r slide to the bad point. Converges or diverges exactly as before.

        ∫_(r)^(1) 1/(√x) dx = 2 − 2√r → 2: the graph has no top near 0, yet the area is 2.

        Harmonic integral (p-integral)

        • ∫_(1)^(∞) 1/(x^(p)) dx converges when p > 1 and diverges when p ≤ 1
        • Near 0, the other way round: ∫_(0)^(1) 1/(x^(p)) dx converges when p < 1 and diverges when p ≥ 1

        Why, for p ≠ 1: ∫_(1)^(r) x^(−p) dx = (r^(1 − p) − 1)/(1 − p). If p > 1 the power 1 − p is negative, so r^(1 − p) → 0 and the limit is 1/(p − 1). If p < 1 it grows to infinity.

        The harmonic case p = 1 diverges: ∫_(1)^(r) 1/(x) dx = ln r → ∞. It is the border: 1/x falls, but just too slowly.

        Why it matters: these are the reference functions for the comparison test (next card).

        1/x (blue) and 1/x² (green) look alike, but the area under 1/x is infinite and the area under 1/x² is 1.

        Comparison test for improper integrals

        0 ≤ f(x) ≤ g(x) for all x ≥ a:

        • ∫_(a)^(∞) g(x) dx converges  ⇒  ∫_(a)^(∞) f(x) dx converges
        • ∫_(a)^(∞) f(x) dx diverges  ⇒  ∫_(a)^(∞) g(x) dx diverges

        In words: under a finite area, a smaller area is finite too. Above an infinite area, a bigger area is infinite too.

        What tells you nothing: the bigger one diverges, or the smaller one converges. Something below an infinite area can be finite or infinite, and something above a finite area too. If you land there, compare with a different function, in the other direction.

        Both conditions matter: f ≥ 0 (no negative parts) and the inequality on the whole [a, ∞), or at least from some point on.

        The same works for unbounded functions near a bad point, with ∫_(0)^(1) 1/(x^(p)) dx as the reference.
