2.1Identities

What an identity is

An identity is an equation that is true for every angle where both sides are defined. Example: sin²θ + cos²θ = 1.

Compare it with an ordinary equation like sin θ = 1/2, which is true only for some angles. An identity is never something you solve. It's a tool: it lets you swap one expression for another that's equal to it, so you can simplify an expression, solve an equation, or find an exact value.

Every identity on these cards comes from the unit circle (2.2): the point at angle θ is (cos θ, sin θ). Use the Identity checker below to see both sides give the same number for any angle.

Pythagorean identities

  • sin²θ + cos²θ = 1
  • 1 + tan²θ = 1/(cos²θ)  (cos θ ≠ 0)
  • 1 + cot²θ = 1/(sin²θ)  (sin θ ≠ 0)

Why it's true: the point on the unit circle makes a right triangle with legs cos θ and sin θ and hypotenuse 1 (the radius). Pythagoras says leg² + leg² = hypotenuse², and that's the first identity.

The other two come from the first: divide every term by cos²θ to get tan²θ + 1 = 1/(cos²θ), or by sin²θ to get 1 + cot²θ = 1/(sin²θ).

Use it to swap: sin²θ = 1 − cos²θ and cos²θ = 1 − sin²θ. That's how an equation with both sin and cos becomes an equation in one of them.

Quotient and reciprocal identities

  • tan θ = (sin θ)/(cos θ)  ·  cot θ = (cos θ)/(sin θ) = 1/(tan θ)
  • sec θ = 1/(cos θ)  ·  csc θ = 1/(sin θ)

These are really the definitions of the other four functions: everything is built from sin and cos. Each one is undefined where its bottom is 0: tan and sec where cos θ = 0 (at 90°, 270°, …), cot and csc where sin θ = 0 (at 0°, 180°, …).

Tip: when an expression looks messy, rewrite everything in sin and cos first. Usually things cancel. See the method "Simplifying with identities".

Sum and difference formulas

  • sin(A + B) = sin A cos B + cos A sin B
  • sin(A − B) = sin A cos B − cos A sin B
  • cos(A + B) = cos A cos B − sin A sin B
  • cos(A − B) = cos A cos B + sin A sin B
  • tan(A ± B) = (tan A ± tan B)/(1 ∓ tan A tan B)

Careful: sin(A + B) is not sin A + sin B. Check with A = B = 90°: sin 180° = 0, but sin 90° + sin 90° = 2.

How to remember them: sin mixes the two functions (sin·cos + cos·sin) and keeps the sign. cos keeps them together (cos·cos, sin·sin) and flips the sign.

Example: cos 75° = cos(45° + 30°) = cos 45° cos 30° − sin 45° sin 30° = (√2)/2 · (√3)/2 − (√2)/2 · 1/2 = (√6 − √2)/4.

Double-angle formulas

  • sin 2θ = 2 sin θ cos θ
  • cos 2θ = cos²θ − sin²θ = 2cos²θ − 1 = 1 − 2sin²θ
  • tan 2θ = (2 tan θ)/(1 − tan²θ)

Where they come from: put A = B = θ in the sum formulas. sin(θ + θ) = sin θ cos θ + cos θ sin θ = 2 sin θ cos θ.

Three versions of cos 2θ: the first one, with sin²θ = 1 − cos²θ swapped in, gives the second; with cos²θ = 1 − sin²θ, the third. Pick the one that leaves only the function you want: only cos, or only sin.

Example: solve sin 2x = sin x. Write 2 sin x cos x = sin x, then sin x (2cos x − 1) = 0, so sin x = 0 or cos x = 1/2. (Never divide by sin x: you'd lose the solutions where it's 0.)

Half-angle formulas

  • sin²(θ/2) = (1 − cos θ)/2
  • cos²(θ/2) = (1 + cos θ)/2

Where they come from: the double-angle formula cos 2α = 1 − 2sin²α, solved for sin²α, with α = θ/2. Same for cos² from cos 2α = 2cos²α − 1.

They lower a power: a square becomes a first power of a double angle. That's why they're useful for integrals like ∫ sin²x dx.

Example: sin²15° = (1 − cos 30°)/2 = (1 − (√3)/2)/2 = (2 − √3)/4. Since sin 15° > 0, take the positive root: sin 15° = (√(2 − √3))/2.

Co-function and even/odd identities

  • sin(90° − θ) = cos θ  ·  cos(90° − θ) = sin θ
  • cos(−θ) = cos θ (cos is even)
  • sin(−θ) = −sin θ, tan(−θ) = −tan θ (sin and tan are odd)

Co-function: a right triangle's two acute angles add up to 90°. The side opposite θ is the side adjacent to 90° − θ, so the sine of one angle is the cosine of the other. ("Co-sine" literally means "sine of the complementary angle".)

Even/odd: the angle −θ is θ measured clockwise. Its point is the mirror image across the x-axis: same x (so cos stays the same), opposite y (so sin flips sign). tan = sin/cos then flips too.

2.2The unit circle

The unit circle

The unit circle is the circle of radius 1 around the origin. For an angle θ, measured counter-clockwise from the positive x-axis, the point on the circle is (cos θ, sin θ).

In words: cos θ is how far right (or left, if negative) the point is, and sin θ is how far up (or down). Because the radius is 1, these are the same as "adjacent / hypotenuse" and "opposite / hypotenuse" in a right triangle, but now they work for any angle, even 200° or −30°.

Why this is useful: both values are always between −1 and 1, and you can read their signs straight from the picture. Turning one full circle brings you back to the same point, which is why sin and cos repeat (2.5).

Signs in the four quadrants

QI: all positive · QII: only sin positive · QIII: only tan positive · QIV: only cos positive.

Why: cos is the x-coordinate, so it's positive on the right (I, IV). sin is the y-coordinate, so it's positive on top (I, II). tan = sin/cos is positive when both have the same sign (I, III).

Memory aid: "All Students Take Calculus", going I → II → III → IV: All, Sin, Tan, Cos.

Where tan θ sits on the circle

Draw the vertical line x = 1 (it touches the circle at (1, 0)). Extend the radius until it meets that line. The height of the meeting point is tan θ.

Why: the small triangle (legs cos θ, sin θ) and the big one (legs 1, tan θ) have the same angles, so their sides are in the same ratio: tan θ / 1 = sin θ / cos θ. That vertical line is a tangent to the circle, which is where the name "tangent" comes from.

At 90° the radius points straight up and never meets the line, which is why tan 90° is undefined.

Reference angles: values past 90°

The reference angle is the acute angle between the radius and the x-axis. sin, cos, tan of any angle equal those of its reference angle, up to a sign that the quadrant decides.

Example: 150° is 30° short of 180°, so its reference angle is 30°. Its point is the mirror image of the 30° point: same height, opposite side. So sin 150° = sin 30° = 1/2 and cos 150° = −cos 30° = −(√3)/2.

Finding the reference angle (for 0° ≤ θ < 360°): QII: 180° − θ · QIII: θ − 180° · QIV: 360° − θ. The full steps are the method "Exact value of any angle".

2.3Radians

What a radian is

An angle of 1 radian cuts off an arc whose length equals the radius. A full turn is 360° = 2π rad, so 180° = π rad.

Degrees → radians: multiply by π/180. Radians → degrees: multiply by 180/π.

Why use radians? They measure an angle by a length: on the unit circle the angle in radians is the arc length. That makes calculus formulas clean: (sin x)' = cos x is true only when x is in radians.

Why 2π? The whole circumference is 2πr, so 2π radius-lengths fit around the circle.

Example: 45° = 45 · π/180 = π/4. And (5π)/6 = (5π)/6 · 180/π = 150°.

arcsin k is the angle in [−π/2, π/2] whose sine is k. arccos k is the angle in [0, π] whose cosine is k. arctan k is the angle in (−π/2, π/2) whose tangent is k.

One answer, not all answers

arcsin, arccos and arctan give one angle (the principal value). An equation like sin x = k usually has infinitely many solutions.

Example: arcsin(1/2) = π/6, but sin x = 1/2 is also true at (5π)/6, and at both of those plus any number of full turns. That's why the general solutions in 2.6 add + 2πn and a second, mirrored family.

Calculator warning: a calculator's sin⁻¹ button is arcsin. It shows only the principal value, never the second family.

2.5Periodicity

Period of sin, cos and tan

sin(θ + 2πn) = sin θ and cos(θ + 2πn) = cos θ: period 2π (360°).

tan(θ + πn) = tan θ: period π (180°). Here n is any integer.

Why: adding 2π to an angle is one full turn around the unit circle, back to the same point, so sin and cos come back to the same values. For tan, half a turn is enough: the point moves to the opposite side of the circle, both coordinates flip sign, and sin/cos stays the same.

Why it matters: an equation like sin x = 1/2 has a solution in every period, so its answer is a whole family: x = π/6 + 2πn, and so on (2.6).

An inner coefficient changes the period: sin(Bx) repeats every (2π)/(|B|). Example: sin 3x has period (2π)/3. (More in Functions › 3.)

2.6Solving trig equations

General solutions

  • sin x = k → x = arcsin k + 2πn  or  x = π − arcsin k + 2πn
  • cos x = k → x = arccos k + 2πn  or  x = −arccos k + 2πn
  • tan x = k → x = arctan k + πn

n is any integer. For sin and cos there is no solution when k > 1 or k < −1.

Why two families for sin: sin x is the height on the unit circle. A horizontal line at height k crosses the circle at two points, mirror images across the y-axis: one at arcsin k, the other at π − arcsin k. Then + 2πn adds every full turn.

Why two families for cos: cos x is the x-coordinate. A vertical line at x = k crosses the circle at two points, mirror images across the x-axis: arccos k and −arccos k. That's why it's written x = ± arccos k + 2πn.

Why one family for tan: its period is π, and + πn already reaches both points (they're opposite each other on the circle).

AAbsolute value

What absolute value means

|x| is the distance of x from 0 on the number line, so it's never negative.

As a rule: |x| = x when x ≥ 0, and |x| = −x when x < 0.

In words: absolute value removes the sign. |5| = 5 and |−5| = 5, because both are 5 steps from zero.

Why "−x" for negatives: if x is negative, then −x is positive. For x = −5: −x = −(−5) = 5. The minus sign isn't making the answer negative; it's cancelling the negative.

Distance between two numbers: |a − b| is the distance between a and b. So |x − 2| = 3 means "x is 3 steps from 2": x = 5 or x = −1.

Distance a from 0

Properties of absolute value

  • |a| ≥ 0, and |a| = 0 only when a = 0
  • |−a| = |a|
  • |a · b| = |a| · |b|  ·  |a/b| = (|a|)/(|b|) (b ≠ 0)
  • √(a²) = |a|
  • |a + b| ≤ |a| + |b| (the triangle inequality)

The one that catches people: √(x²) is not always x. For x = −3: √((−3)²) = √9 = 3, which is |−3|, not −3. A square root always gives the non-negative answer.

Multiplying is fine, adding is not: |−2 · 3| = 6 = |−2| · |3|. But |−2 + 3| = 1, while |−2| + |3| = 5. Adding can only make it smaller or equal, never bigger: that's the triangle inequality.

BInequalities

Inequalities and interval notation

The answer to an inequality is usually a whole range of numbers, written as an interval:

  • [a, b]: a ≤ x ≤ b (ends included) · (a, b): a < x < b (ends left out)
  • [a, b): a ≤ x < b · (a, ∞): x > a · (−∞, b]: x ≤ b

Square bracket or round? Square [ ] and a filled dot mean the end number is part of the answer (≤, ≥). Round ( ) and an empty dot mean it isn't (<, >). Infinity always gets a round bracket, because it's not a number you can reach.

Solving works almost like an equation: you can add or subtract the same thing on both sides, and multiply or divide by a positive number. The only new rule is about negative numbers (next card).

Multiplying or dividing by a number

Multiply or divide both sides by a positive number: the inequality keeps its direction. By a negative number: it flips (< becomes >).

Why it flips: multiplying by −1 mirrors the number line around 0. The number that was further right ends up further left. 2 < 3, but −2 > −3 (−2 is closer to zero, so it's bigger).

Example: −3x < 12. Divide by −3 and flip: x > −4. Check with x = 0: 0 < 12 ✓.

Dividing by an expression of unknown sign

You may not multiply or divide an inequality by something like x or x − 1 when you don't know its sign. Two safe ways instead:

  • Sign chart (usually easier): move everything to one side, make one fraction, and find where it's positive or negative.
  • Case split: solve once assuming the expression is positive, once assuming it's negative (and flip), then join the two answers with OR.

Why you can't just multiply: if x is negative, the inequality must flip; if it's positive, it mustn't. Not knowing which, you'd be guessing. Example of the mistake: from 1/(x) > 2, multiplying by x gives 1 > 2x, so x < ½. But x = −1 fits that and fails the original (−1 > 2 is false).

The right answer, by sign chart: 1/(x) − 2 > 0 → (1 − 2x)/(x) > 0. The top is 0 at x = ½, the bottom at x = 0. Test one number in each piece: the fraction is positive only between them. Answer: 0 < x < ½. Steps: the methods "Solving an inequality with a sign chart" and "Case split for an unknown sign".

Quadratic inequalities

For ax² + bx + c < 0 or > 0: find the roots, sketch the parabola, and read where it's below or above the x-axis.

  • a > 0 (opens up), two roots x₁ < x₂: negative between the roots, positive outside them.
  • No roots: the parabola is entirely above (a > 0) or below (a < 0) the axis.

Why the roots matter: the parabola can only change sign where it crosses the axis, at the roots. Between two roots the sign is the same everywhere, so one test number tells you the sign of the whole piece.

Example: x² − 5x + 4 < 0. Factor: (x − 1)(x − 4) < 0, roots 1 and 4. The parabola opens up, so it's below the axis between them: 1 < x < 4. For ≥ 0 it would be x ≤ 1 or x ≥ 4.

Don't take a square root of both sides: x² < 9 is not x < 3 (that lets in x = −10). It's −3 < x < 3.

Square roots in inequalities

First the domain: what's under a root must be ≥ 0. Then:

  • √(f(x)) < k: if k ≤ 0, no solution. If k > 0: f(x) ≥ 0 AND f(x) < k².
  • √(f(x)) > k: if k < 0, the whole domain f(x) ≥ 0. If k ≥ 0: f(x) > k².
  • √A < √B: A ≥ 0 AND A < B.

Why the domain comes first: squaring both sides hides the root, and with it the warning that the inside can't be negative. In √(x − 2) < 3, squaring gives x − 2 < 9, so x < 11. But x = 0 fits that, and √(−2) doesn't exist. Adding the domain x ≥ 2 fixes it: 2 ≤ x < 11, exactly the green piece in the sketch.

Why squaring is allowed here: both sides are ≥ 0 (a root and a positive k), and for non-negative numbers, a < b exactly when a² < b². With a negative side that fails: −5 < 1 but 25 > 1.

CWhen to use "and" vs "or"

AND or OR: which one?

AND: every condition must be true at the same x. Keep only the overlap (the intersection, ∩).

OR: any one condition is enough. Keep everything that any of them covers (the union, ∪).

Use AND when: a domain condition meets the solved inequality (x ≥ 2 and x < 11); a double inequality like −k < E < k; a case assumption with the result solved under it (x > 0 and x < ½).

Use OR when: the two answers of an absolute value equation (x = 5 or x = −2); the two pieces of |E| > k; joining the answers of different cases.

A quick test: ask "does one condition alone make x a solution?" Yes → OR. No, it needs all of them → AND.

1Angles from parallel lines & a transversal

Angles at a point and on a line

  • Angles that together make a straight line add up to 180°.
  • Angles all the way around a point add up to 360°.
  • Vertical angles (opposite each other where two lines cross) are equal.

Why vertical angles are equal: a and b sit next to each other on a straight line, so a + b = 180°. The angle opposite a also sits next to b, so it's also 180° − b. Both equal 180° − b, so they're equal.

Angle pairs with parallel lines

A transversal is a line that crosses two other lines. When those two lines are parallel:

  • Corresponding angles (same position at each crossing) are equal: 1 = 5.
  • Alternate interior angles (between the lines, on opposite sides of the transversal) are equal: 3 = 5, 4 = 6.
  • Alternate exterior angles (outside the lines, opposite sides) are equal: 1 = 7.
  • Co-interior angles (between the lines, same side) add up to 180°: 4 + 5 = 180°.

The short version: with parallel lines there are only two different angle sizes. All the sharp (acute) angles are equal, all the wide (obtuse) ones are equal, and one of each adds up to 180°. In the sketch: 1, 3, 5, 7 are one size, 2, 4, 6, 8 the other.

Why: sliding the top line down along the transversal (it stays parallel) lands it on the bottom line, so the angles at the top crossing match those at the bottom one.

The converse is how you prove lines are parallel: if any of these pairs is equal (or co-interior angles add up to 180°), the lines are parallel.

2Triangles

Angle sum and the triangle inequality

  • The three angles of a triangle add up to 180°.
  • Triangle inequality: each side is shorter than the other two together: a < b + c.
  • The longest side is opposite the biggest angle (and the shortest opposite the smallest).

Why 180°: draw a line through the top corner, parallel to the base. The angle on its left equals α and the angle on its right equals β (alternate interior angles, Geometry › 1). Together with γ they make a straight line: α + β + γ = 180°.

Why the triangle inequality: the straight path from one corner to another is the shortest. Going around through the third corner is longer. Example: sides 2, 3, 6 can't make a triangle, since 2 + 3 < 6: the two short sides can't reach.

Set the three sides

Exterior angle and midsegment

  • An exterior angle equals the sum of the two interior angles that aren't next to it. The exterior angles (one at each corner) add up to 360°.
  • A midsegment (joining the midpoints of two sides) is parallel to the third side and half as long.

Why the exterior angle works: δ and β lie on a straight line, so δ = 180° − β. The angle sum gives α + γ = 180° − β too. So δ = α + γ.

Example: if the base is 10, the midsegment is 5. This is a special case of Thales' theorem (Geometry › 5) with ratio 1/2.

Special lines and centres of a triangle

  • Median: from a corner to the midpoint of the opposite side. The three medians meet at the centroid, which cuts each median 2 : 1 (the longer part at the corner).
  • Altitude (height): from a corner, perpendicular to the opposite side. They meet at the orthocentre.
  • Angle bisector: splits a corner's angle in half. They meet at the incentre, the centre of the circle that touches all three sides.
  • Perpendicular bisector: through a side's midpoint, perpendicular to it. They meet at the circumcentre, the centre of the circle through all three corners.

Don't mix them up: a median goes to the middle of a side (it doesn't have to be perpendicular); an altitude is perpendicular (it doesn't have to hit the middle). In an isosceles triangle, from the top corner, they're the same line, and also the angle bisector.

Area of a triangle

  • S = (base · height)/2
  • S = (1/2) · a · b · sin C
  • S = √(s(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)),   s = (a + b + c)/2 (Heron)

Why half: a triangle is half of a rectangle (or parallelogram) with the same base and height.

Which one to use: base and height known → the first. Two sides and the angle between them → the second (b · sin C is exactly the height). All three sides → Heron.

Example: sides 5 and 8 with 30° between them: S = (1/2) · 5 · 8 · sin 30° = 20 · 1/2 = 10. Heron for sides 3, 4, 5: s = 6, S = √(6 · 3 · 2 · 1) = √36 = 6.

Congruent and similar triangles

Congruent (same shape and size) if they match by: SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, or HL (right triangles: hypotenuse and a leg). SSA does not prove it.

Similar (same shape, any size) if: two angles are equal (AA), all three sides are in the same ratio (SSS~), or two sides are in the same ratio with the angle between them equal (SAS~).

What the letters mean: S = a side, A = an angle, in the order you walk around the triangle. SAS = two sides and the angle between them. That "between" matters: with the angle not between the sides (SSA), two different triangles can fit (Geometry › 6, the ambiguous case).

Similar triangles and ratios: every length grows by the same ratio k (sides, heights, medians, perimeter). Areas grow by k². In the sketch, k = 1.5, so the big triangle's area is 1.5² = 2.25 times the small one's.

Types of triangles

By sides: scalene (all different), isosceles (two equal), equilateral (all equal). By angles: acute (all under 90°), right (one is 90°), obtuse (one over 90°).

    Angles of a polygon

    • Interior angles: (n − 2) · 180°
    • Exterior angles: always 360°
    • Diagonals: (n(n − 3))/2

    Here n is the number of sides of a convex polygon. A regular polygon (all sides and angles equal) has each interior angle ((n − 2) · 180°)/(n).

    Why n − 2: from one corner, draw every diagonal. They cut the polygon into n − 2 triangles, and each triangle's angles add up to 180°.

    Why the exterior angles make 360°: walk around the polygon. At each corner you turn by the exterior angle, and after one lap you've turned exactly once around: 360°.

    Example: a regular hexagon (n = 6): sum 4 · 180° = 720°, each angle 720°/6 = 120°, diagonals (6 · 3)/2 = 9.

    Types of quadrilaterals

    The angles of any quadrilateral add up to 360° (it splits into 2 triangles).

    • Parallelogram: both pairs of opposite sides parallel. Opposite sides and opposite angles are equal; the diagonals cut each other in half.
    • Rectangle: a parallelogram with four right angles; equal diagonals.
    • Rhombus: a parallelogram with four equal sides; perpendicular diagonals.
    • Square: both a rectangle and a rhombus.
    • Trapezoid: exactly one pair of parallel sides (the bases).
    • Kite: two pairs of equal neighbouring sides; perpendicular diagonals.

    They're a family: every square is a rectangle and a rhombus, and every rectangle and rhombus is a parallelogram. So a square has every property on this list (except the trapezoid's). Pick a type below to see it.

      4Circles

      Circumference, area and arcs

      • Circumference: C = 2πr  ·  Area: S = πr²
      • Arc of angle α°: (α/360°) · 2πr  ·  Sector: (α/360°) · πr²
      • In radians: arc = rθ, sector = (r²θ)/2

      An arc and a sector are fractions of the whole: an angle of 90° is a quarter of 360°, so its arc is a quarter of the circumference and its sector a quarter of the area.

      Example: r = 6, α = 60°. Arc: (60/360) · 12π = 2π. Sector: (60/360) · 36π = 6π.

      Inscribed and central angles

      A central angle has its corner at the centre. An inscribed angle has its corner on the circle. On the same arc, the inscribed angle is half the central angle.

      • Inscribed angles on the same arc are equal.
      • An inscribed angle on a diameter (a half circle) is 90°.

      Why it's useful: wherever P sits on the big arc, the angle APB stays the same. So if you know one angle on an arc, you know them all. In the sketch the central angle is 140° and every inscribed angle on that arc is 70°.

      Half circle: a diameter is a central angle of 180°, so any angle "looking at" the diameter from the circle is 90°. That's the quickest way to find a right angle in a circle.

      Chords

      • A line from the centre perpendicular to a chord cuts the chord (and its arc) in half. And the other way round: the line from the centre to a chord's midpoint is perpendicular to it.
      • Equal chords are the same distance from the centre. The longer the chord, the closer it is to the centre.
      • Two chords crossing at P: AP · PB = CP · PD.

      Why: OA and OB are both radii, so triangle OAB is isosceles. In an isosceles triangle the height from the top is also the median, so it hits AB at its middle.

      Example: radius 5, chord 8. Half the chord is 4, so by Pythagoras the distance from the centre is √(5² − 4²) = 3.

      Tangents and the power of a point

      • A tangent touches the circle at one point and is perpendicular to the radius there.
      • Two tangents from one outside point have equal lengths: PA = PB. The line from P to the centre cuts the angle between them in half.
      • The angle between a tangent and a chord is half the arc it cuts off.
      • From an outside point P: two secants: PA · PB = PC · PD; a tangent and a secant: PT² = PA · PB.

      Why the two tangents are equal: triangles OAP and OBP both have a right angle (tangent ⊥ radius), equal radii OA = OB, and the same hypotenuse OP. So they're congruent (HL), and PA = PB.

      Example: the distance from P to the centre is 13, the radius is 5. The tangent length is √(13² − 5²) = 12.

      Cyclic quadrilaterals

      A quadrilateral with all four corners on one circle: its opposite angles add up to 180°. And the other way round: if opposite angles add up to 180°, a circle passes through all four corners.

      Why: ∠A and ∠C are inscribed angles on the two arcs between B and D. Those arcs make the whole circle, 360°, and each angle is half its arc, so together they're 180°.

      An exterior angle of a cyclic quadrilateral equals the interior angle at the opposite corner.

      5Thales' theorem

      Thales' theorem in short

      Parallel lines cut the sides of an angle into proportional pieces. In triangle ABC, with DE ∥ BC:

      • Small Thales: (AD)/(DB) = (AE)/(EC)
      • Big Thales: (AD)/(AB) = (AE)/(AC) = (DE)/(BC)
      • Hourglass, with AB ∥ DE crossing at P: (PA)/(PD) = (PB)/(PE) = (AB)/(DE)
      • Parallel lines cutting two lines: (AB)/(BC) = (A'B')/(B'C')

      Why it's true: triangles ADE and ABC have the same angles (the corner at A is shared, and the parallel lines make the other angles equal), so they're similar. Similar triangles have all sides in the same ratio.

      Small or big? Small Thales compares the two pieces of each side (AD with DB). Big Thales compares a piece with the whole side (AD with AB), and only big Thales includes DE/BC. Mixing them up is the most common mistake.

      Example: AD = 4, DB = 6, AE = 5. Small Thales: 4/6 = 5/(EC), so EC = 7.5.

      The converse: if the ratios are equal, the lines are parallel.

        Right triangle facts

        • Pythagoras: a² + b² = c², where c is the hypotenuse.
        • Height to the hypotenuse: h² = p · q, where p and q are the two pieces it cuts the hypotenuse into.
        • The median to the hypotenuse is half the hypotenuse, c/2; it's the radius of the circle through all three corners.
        • Special triangles: 45°–45°–90° has sides 1 : 1 : √2; 30°–60°–90° has sides 1 : √3 : 2.

        Why h² = p · q: the height splits the triangle into two smaller triangles that are similar to each other, so h/p = q/h.

        Why the median is half the hypotenuse: a right angle is an inscribed angle on a diameter (Geometry › 4), so the hypotenuse is a diameter and its midpoint is the centre. All three corners are one radius away from it.

        Example: legs 6 and 8: hypotenuse √(36 + 64) = 10, median to it 5.

        • Law of sines: (a)/(sin A) = (b)/(sin B) = (c)/(sin C) = 2R

        Side a is opposite angle A, and so on. R is the radius of the circle through the three corners.

        1Powers

        What a power is

        aⁿ = a · a · … · a (n times). a is the base, n the exponent.

        • a⁰ = 1  ·  a⁻ⁿ = 1/(aⁿ)  (a ≠ 0)
        • a^(1/n) = ⁿ√a  ·  a^(m/n) = ⁿ√(aᵐ)

        Why a⁰ = 1: go down the list 2³ = 8, 2² = 4, 2¹ = 2. Each step down divides by 2. One more step: 2⁰ = 2/2 = 1. Keep going: 2⁻¹ = 1/2, 2⁻² = 1/4. That's why a negative exponent means "one over".

        Why a fraction exponent is a root: (9^(1/2))² = 9^(1/2 · 2) = 9¹ = 9, so 9^(1/2) is the number whose square is 9: √9 = 3. Likewise 8^(2/3) = (∛8)² = 2² = 4.

        Careful with roots: an even root of a negative number isn't a real number, so with an even n you need a ≥ 0.

        Rules of powers

        • aᵐ · aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ  ·  (aᵐ)/(aⁿ) = aᵐ⁻ⁿ  ·  (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ
        • (ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ  ·  (a/b)ⁿ = (aⁿ)/(bⁿ)

        Why they work, by counting: a³ · a² = (a·a·a)·(a·a) is five a's: a⁵. (a³)² = a³ · a³ is six: a⁶. Dividing cancels: (a⁵)/(a²) = a³.

        Classic mistakes:

        • (a + b)² is not a² + b²; it's a² + 2ab + b². The rules only work for multiplying and dividing, never for adding.
        • 2³ · 3² can't be combined: the bases are different.
        • −2² is −4 (the power comes first), but (−2)² = 4.

        Example: (4³ · 2⁻¹)/(8) = (2⁶ · 2⁻¹)/(2³) = 2^(6 − 1 − 3) = 2² = 4. Writing everything with the same base (here 2) is the trick.

        The exponential function y = aˣ

        For a base a > 0, a ≠ 1:

        • It's defined for every x, and always positive: aˣ > 0.
        • It passes through (0, 1), since a⁰ = 1.
        • a > 1: increasing. 0 < a < 1: decreasing.
        • It's one-to-one: aˣ = aʸ only when x = y.

        What "one-to-one" buys you: if two powers of the same base are equal, their exponents are equal. That's the whole idea behind solving 2^(x + 1) = 2⁵: x + 1 = 5.

        What "increasing" buys you: for a > 1, a bigger exponent gives a bigger value, so inequalities keep their direction. For 0 < a < 1 the graph goes down, so they flip.

        Exponential equations: same base

        Write both sides as powers of the same base, then set the exponents equal: a^(f(x)) = a^(g(x)) → f(x) = g(x).

        Why it's allowed: y = aˣ is one-to-one, so equal values mean equal exponents.

        What a logarithm is

        log_a(b) is the exponent you put on a to get b:

        log_a(b) = c  ⟺  aᶜ = b  (a > 0, a ≠ 1, b > 0)

        • log_a(1) = 0  ·  log_a(a) = 1
        • a^(log_a(b)) = b  ·  log_a(aᵏ) = k

        In words: log₂8 asks "2 to what power gives 8?" The answer is 3, because 2³ = 8. A log is an exponent. log₁₀1000 = 3, log₃(1/9) = −2.

        Log undoes power: log_a and aˣ cancel each other. That's why their graphs are mirror images across the line y = x: swap the coordinates of a point on one and you get a point on the other.

        Why b > 0: a positive base to any power is positive, so no power gives 0 or a negative number. log₂(−4) doesn't exist. That's where log domains come from.

        Notation: log with no base usually means base 10, and ln means base e ≈ 2.718.

        Rules of logarithms

        • log_a(M · N) = log_a(M) + log_a(N)
        • log_a(M/N) = log_a(M) − log_a(N)
        • log_a(Mᵏ) = k · log_a(M)
        • Change of base: log_a(b) = (log_c(b))/(log_c(a))  ·  log_a(b) = 1/(log_b(a))

        Why: logs are exponents, so they follow the power rules. Multiplying powers adds exponents (2³ · 2⁴ = 2⁷), so the log of a product is the sum of the logs.

        Turning a number into a log lets it join the other terms: 2 = log₃(9), so log₃(x) + 2 = log₃(x) + log₃(9) = log₃(9x).

        Classic mistakes: log(M + N) is not log M + log N (nothing simplifies a log of a sum). And (log M)/(log N) is not log M − log N.

        Example: log₂(40) − log₂(5) = log₂(40/5) = log₂(8) = 3. And on a calculator: log₂(5) = (ln 5)/(ln 2) ≈ 2.32.

        Even powers inside a log

        log_a(x²) = 2 · log_a|x|, defined for every x ≠ 0.

        Why the absolute value: log_a(x²) works for negative x too, since x² is positive. But 2 · log_a(x) needs x > 0. Writing it without |·| quietly throws away all the negative x values. The absolute value keeps them.

        Rule of thumb: when you pull an even exponent out of a log, put |·| around what's left. With an odd exponent it isn't needed: log_a(x³) already needs x > 0.

        Example: solve log₂(x²) = 4. Correct: x² = 16, so x = 4 or x = −4 (both work). Wrong: 2 log₂(x) = 4 gives only x = 4.

        Increasing, decreasing and sign charts

        f'(x) > 0 on an interval → f is increasing there. f'(x) < 0 → decreasing. The points where f' = 0 or f' is undefined are the critical points: the only places the direction can change.

        Why: f' is the slope of the tangent. A positive slope means the graph is going up as you move right.

        Antiderivatives and signed area

        If F' = f, then F is an antiderivative of f and ∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + C.

        ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a) is the signed area between the graph and the x-axis, from a to b: area above the axis counts plus, area below counts minus.

        In words: integrating is differentiating backwards. Since (x³)' = 3x², an antiderivative of 3x² is x³. The + C is there because constants disappear when you differentiate, so x³ + 5 works too.

        Example: ∫_(0)^(2) x² dx = (2³)/3 − (0³)/3 = 8/3. Try x² with a = 0 and b = 2 below.

        The integral counts area below the axis as negative. For the real area, split the interval where f = 0, integrate each piece, and add up the pieces' absolute values.

        Substitution: undoing the chain rule

        When an integral contains an inner expression and its derivative, set t = the inner expression. Then dt = t'(x) dx, and the integral becomes a simple one in t.

        Shortcut: ∫ g'(x) · (g(x))ⁿ dx = ((g(x))ⁿ⁺¹)/(n + 1) + C  (n ≠ −1)

        Why it works: the chain rule says ((x² + 1)⁵)' = 5(x² + 1)⁴ · 2x. Read backwards: an integral with (x² + 1)⁴ and a 2x next to it came from (x² + 1)⁵.

        Example, step by step: ∫ 2x(x² + 1)⁴ dx. (1) t = x² + 1. (2) dt = 2x dx: that's exactly the rest of the integral. (3) It becomes ∫ t⁴ dt = (t⁵)/5 + C. (4) Put x back: ((x² + 1)⁵)/5 + C.

        • (sin x)' = cos x  ·  (cos x)' = −sin x  ·  (tan x)' = 1/(cos²x)
        • With an inside g(x): (sin g)' = cos g · g'  ·  (cos g)' = −sin g · g'  ·  (tan g)' = (g')/(cos²g)

        See it in the graphs: at the top of the sine wave (x = π/2) the tangent is flat, so the derivative is 0, and indeed cos(π/2) = 0. At x = 0 sine rises fastest, with slope 1, and cos 0 = 1. The cosine curve is the slope of the sine curve.

        Example: (sin(3x²))' = cos(3x²) · 6x. The outside (sin) becomes cos with the same inside, times the inside's derivative.

        These need x in radians.

        Integrals of sin and cos

        • ∫ sin x dx = −cos x + C
        • ∫ cos x dx = sin x + C
        • ∫ 1/(cos²x) dx = tan x + C
        • Inside ax + b: ∫ cos(ax + b) dx = (1/a) sin(ax + b) + C

        Where the minus comes from: read the derivatives backwards. (cos x)' = −sin x, so to get +sin x you need −cos x. Check by differentiating: (−cos x)' = sin x ✓.

        Why 1/a: differentiating sin(ax + b) gives an extra a (chain rule), so the integral must divide by it. ∫ cos 3x dx = (1/3) sin 3x + C.

        More: Integrals › 4 (Sine and cosine).

        4Exponential & logarithmic calculus

        Derivatives and integrals of eˣ and ln x

        • (eˣ)' = eˣ  ·  (aˣ)' = aˣ · ln a
        • (ln x)' = 1/(x)  ·  (log_a(x))' = 1/(x · ln a)
        • With an inside g: (e^(g))' = e^(g) · g'  ·  (ln g)' = (g')/(g)
        • ∫ eˣ dx = eˣ + C  ·  ∫ aˣ dx = (aˣ)/(ln a) + C
        • ∫ 1/(x) dx = ln|x| + C

        eˣ is its own derivative: at every point, the slope of its graph equals its height. That's what makes e special (next card).

        Why ln|x|: 1/(x) is defined for negative x too, but ln x isn't. ln|x| is defined for every x ≠ 0 and its derivative is still 1/(x).

        Examples: (e^(3x))' = 3e^(3x). (ln(x² + 1))' = (2x)/(x² + 1). ∫ 2ˣ dx = (2ˣ)/(ln 2) + C.

        The number e

        e = lim_(n→∞) (1 + 1/n)ⁿ ≈ 2.71828

        It's the one base for which the graph of y = eˣ crosses the y-axis with slope exactly 1.

        Why that matters: for any base, the slope of aˣ is aˣ times its slope at 0. For 2ˣ that slope is about 0.69, for 3ˣ about 1.10. Somewhere between 2 and 3 it's exactly 1, and that base is e. So (eˣ)' = eˣ · 1 = eˣ.

        Where the limit comes from: (1 + 1/n)ⁿ is what 1 grows to if it earns 100% interest per year, added in n small steps. More steps help less and less, and the total settles at e. Watch it below.

        Function

        4Composition of functions

        Composition

        (f∘g)(x) = f(g(x)) — first g (inner), then f (outer) on its result.

        f(x) = x², g(x) = x + 1:  f(g(x)) = (x + 1)²,  g(f(x)) = x² + 1.

        Domain of a composition

        x is in the domain of f∘g ⇔ x is in the domain of g AND g(x) is in the domain of f.

        Find it from these two conditions, not from the simplified formula.

        Rules of composition

        • Order matters: in general f∘g ≠ g∘f
        • Brackets don't: (f∘g)∘h = f∘(g∘h)
        • Identity id(x) = x: f∘id = id∘f = f

        1What is a limit

        Limit of a function

        lim_(x→a) f(x) = L — as x gets close to a (with x ≠ a), f(x) gets as close to L as we want.

        Full notation (ε–δ):

        lim_(x→a) f(x) = L  ⇔  ∀ε > 0 ∃δ > 0 : 0 < |x − a| < δ ⇒ |f(x) − L| < ε

        For every band L ± ε (blue) there is a band a ± δ (green) where the graph — except at a itself — stays inside the blue band.

        One-sided limits

        • lim_(x→a⁻) f(x) — from the left (x < a)  ·  lim_(x→a⁺) f(x) — from the right (x > a)
        • lim_(x→a) f(x) = L  ⇔  lim_(x→a⁻) f(x) = lim_(x→a⁺) f(x) = L

        Left limit L₁ ≠ right limit L₂ → no limit at a. Typical at the breakpoint of a piecewise function or |·| (Functions › 2).

        Properties of a limit

        • The value at the point doesn't matter. f(a) may be anything, or not exist — the limit only looks at x near a.
        • Unique: lim_(x→a) f(x) = L₁ and lim_(x→a) f(x) = L₂ ⇒ L₁ = L₂
        • Local: f(x) = g(x) for all x ≠ a near a ⇒ lim_(x→a) f(x) = lim_(x→a) g(x)  (this is why cancelling works)

        Here lim_(x→a) f(x) = L even though f(a) ≠ L.

        Infinite limits and limits at infinity

        • lim_(x→∞) f(x) = L  ⇔  ∀ε > 0 ∃M : x > M ⇒ |f(x) − L| < ε
        • lim_(x→a) f(x) = ∞  ⇔  ∀M ∃δ > 0 : 0 < |x − a| < δ ⇒ f(x) > M

        A limit of ±∞ is not a finite limit — the arithmetic of limits needs finite ones.

        2Arithmetic of limits

        Arithmetic of limits

        If lim_(x→a) f(x) = L and lim_(x→a) g(x) = M with L, M ∈ ℝ (both exist and are finite), then:

        • lim_(x→a) [f(x) ± g(x)] = L ± M
        • lim_(x→a) c·f(x) = c·L
        • lim_(x→a) [f(x)·g(x)] = L·M
        • lim_(x→a) f(x)/g(x) = L/M  if M ≠ 0

        In words: when both limits exist (finite), you may split the limit over + − · ÷.

        When you can't use the arithmetic

        • One exists, one doesn't ⇒ the sum has no limit: lim f exists, lim g doesn't ⇒ lim (f + g) doesn't (else g = (f + g) − f would have one).
        • Neither exists ⇒ can't tell — the sum or product may still have a limit (examples next card).
        • Indeterminate forms — unknown until you rewrite the expression: 0/0, ∞/∞, ∞ − ∞, 0·∞, 1∞, 00, ∞0
        • Not indeterminate: L/∞ = 0,  ∞ + ∞ = ∞,  L·∞ = ±∞ (L ≠ 0),  L/0 (L ≠ 0) = ±∞ — check the sign from each side.

        No limits apart, a limit together

        Sum — at a = 0: f(x) = 1/x, g(x) = −1/x have no limit, but f(x) + g(x) = 0 → limit 0.

        Product — at a = 0, the sign function

        f(x) = g(x) =1x ≥ 0−1x < 0

        has a jump (no limit), but f(x)·g(x) = 1 → limit 1. Same for f(x)/g(x) = 1.

        So lim (f + g) existing does not mean lim f and lim g exist — never split a limit before you know both parts exist.

        Zero times bounded

        lim_(x→a) f(x) = 0 and ∃K > 0 : |g(x)| ≤ K near a  ⇒  lim_(x→a) f(x)·g(x) = 0

        Even when lim g doesn't exist: lim_(x→0) x·sin(1/x) = 0, since |sin(1/x)| ≤ 1.

        3Calculating limits

        When plugging in works

        f continuous at a  ⇒  lim_(x→a) f(x) = f(a)

        Every elementary function is continuous on its domain: polynomials, rational functions (denominator ≠ 0), roots, sin, cos, tan, aˣ, ln — and their sums, products, quotients and compositions (concepts 6–7).

        Plugging in gives 0/0, ∞/∞… → a is not in the domain: rewrite first.

        Polynomials and the biggest power

        • lim_(x→a) p(x) = p(a)
        • p(x) = aₙxⁿ + … + a₁x + a₀, aₙ ≠ 0:  lim_(x→±∞) p(x) = lim_(x→±∞) aₙxⁿ
        • Rational p(x)/q(x) at ±∞: deg p < deg q → 0 · equal → ratio of the leading coefficients · deg p > deg q → ±∞

        Shortened multiplication formulas

        • a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b)
        • (a ± b)² = a² ± 2ab + b²
        • a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²)
        • a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²)

        Read right-to-left they factor; left-to-right they build the "partner" that cancels a root.

        Multiplying by 1

        Multiplying by A/A (= 1, for A ≠ 0) changes the form, not the value:

        • (1/xⁿ)/(1/xⁿ) — divide top and bottom by the biggest power (x → ∞)
        • (√x + 2)/(√x + 2) — the conjugate: (√x − 2)(√x + 2) = x − 4 removes the root

        4L'Hôpital's rule

        L'Hôpital's rule

        If f, g are differentiable near a (except maybe at a), g'(x) ≠ 0 near a,

        lim_(x→a) f(x) = lim_(x→a) g(x) = 0  (or both ±∞),  and lim_(x→a) f'(x)/g'(x) = L (L finite or ±∞)

        ⇒  lim_(x→a) f(x)/g(x) = lim_(x→a) f'(x)/g'(x) = L

        Also for x → ±∞ and one-sided limits. Only for 0/0 or ∞/∞. Differentiate top and bottom separately — not the quotient rule. Derivatives: Precalc › 1 Derivatives, 3 Trig calculus, 4 Exp & log calculus.

        Other forms into 0/0 or ∞/∞

        • 0·∞:  f·g = f/(1/g)
        • ∞ − ∞:  common denominator, or the conjugate
        • 1∞, 00, ∞0:  fg = eg·ln f → find lim g·ln f

        5Continuity

        Continuity at a point

        f is continuous at a  ⇔  lim_(x→a) f(x) = f(a)

        Full notation: ∀ε > 0 ∃δ > 0 : |x − a| < δ ⇒ |f(x) − f(a)| < ε

        Three conditions: f(a) exists · lim_(x→a) f(x) exists · they are equal. One fails → not continuous:

        One-sided continuity and intervals

        • From the right: lim_(x→a⁺) f(x) = f(a)  ·  from the left: lim_(x→a⁻) f(x) = f(a)
        • On (a, b): continuous at every point.
        • On [a, b]: on (a, b) + from the right at a + from the left at b.

        6Arithmetic of continuity

        Arithmetic of continuous functions

        f, g continuous at a  ⇒  f ± g, c·f, f·g continuous at a, and f/g too if g(a) ≠ 0.

        x² + sin x is continuous everywhere; (x + 1)/(x − 2) for every x ≠ 2.

        Continuous and not continuous

        • f continuous at a, g not  ⇒  f ± g not continuous at a
        • … and if also f(a) ≠ 0  ⇒  f·g, g/f not continuous at a
        • Both not continuous ⇒ can't tell.

        x + sgn x is not continuous at 0; but x·sgn x = |x| is (there f(0) = 0). Proved by contradiction — see the method.

        7Composition and continuity

        Composition of continuous functions

        g continuous at a and f continuous at g(a)  ⇒  f∘g continuous at a

        sin(x²), √(x² + 1), ecos x — continuous wherever defined. Composition itself: Functions › 4.

        Limit of a composition

        • lim_(x→a) g(x) = b and f continuous at b  ⇒  lim_(x→a) f(g(x)) = f(lim_(x→a) g(x)) = f(b)
        • Substitution: lim_(x→a) g(x) = b, g(x) ≠ b near a, lim_(t→b) f(t) = L  ⇒  lim_(x→a) f(g(x)) = L

        The limit goes inside a continuous function: lim_(x→0) cos(x² + π) = cos π = −1. The second line is why a help variable t works.

        8Intermediate value theorem

        Intermediate value theorem

        f continuous on [a, b] and y between f(a) and f(b)  ⇒  ∃c ∈ [a, b] : f(c) = y

        A continuous graph can't get from f(a) to f(b) without crossing every level in between.

        A root between a sign change

        f continuous on [a, b] and f(a)·f(b) < 0  ⇒  ∃c ∈ (a, b) : f(c) = 0

        Says a root exists — not where, not how many. Continuity on the whole [a, b] is a must: 1/x on [−1, 1] changes sign with no root.

        1What is a derivative

        Derivative at a point

        f'(a) = lim_(h→0) (f(a + h) − f(a))/h

        Same limit, written with x = a + h:

        f'(a) = lim_(x→a) (f(x) − f(a))/(x − a)

        If this limit exists and is finite, f is differentiable at a and the limit is the derivative. The derivative function f' gives this number at every x where it exists. Other notation: y', df/dx.

        The quotient is the slope of the secant (green) through two points of the graph. As h → 0 the second point slides to the first, and the secant turns into the tangent (dashed). Try it live in Precalc › Derivatives › Secant → tangent.

        What the derivative tells you

        • Slope: f'(a) is the slope of the tangent to the graph at a.
        • Tangent line: y = f(a) + f'(a)·(x − a)
        • Rate of change: how fast f changes at a. Near a: f(a + h) ≈ f(a) + f'(a)·h
        • Direction: f' > 0 on an interval → rising; f' < 0 → falling; f'(a) = 0 → horizontal tangent (Derivatives › 7).

        From the definition: for f(x) = x², (f(x + h) − f(x))/h = (2xh + h²)/h = 2x + h → 2x. So f'(x) = 2x: the slope at each point of the parabola.

        2Differentiation formulas

        Powers, polynomials and roots

        • (c)' = 0  ·  (c·f)' = c·f'  ·  (f + g)' = f' + g'
        • (xⁿ)' = n·xⁿ⁻¹
        • (√x)' = 1/(2√x)  (x > 0)
        • (ⁿ√x)' = 1/(n·ⁿ√(xⁿ⁻¹))
        • Any real power, x > 0: (xʳ)' = r·xʳ⁻¹. A root or a fraction is a power: 1/x² = x⁻², so (1/x²)' = −2x⁻³ = −2/x³
        • Polynomial, term by term: (4x³ − 5x + 7)' = 12x² − 5

        The slope grows with x: the derivative of x² is the straight line 2x. Differentiating lowers a power by one.

        Why, from the definition

        • Powers: by the binomial formula (x + h)ⁿ = xⁿ + n·xⁿ⁻¹·h + h²·(…), so
        • ((x + h)ⁿ − xⁿ)/h = n·xⁿ⁻¹ + h·(…) → n·xⁿ⁻¹
        • Root (multiply by the conjugate, Limits › 3):
        • With s = √(x + h) + √x: (√(x + h) − √x)/h = ((x + h) − x)/(h·s) = 1/s
        • As h → 0, s → 2√x, so the limit is 1/(2√x).
        • Sum and constant: split the quotient and use the arithmetic of limits.
        • Real power: xʳ = er·ln x, so by the chain rule (xʳ)' = xʳ·(r/x) = r·xʳ⁻¹.

        Product and quotient

        • Product: (f·g)' = f'·g + f·g'
        • Quotient: (f/g)' = (f'·g − f·g')/g²  (g ≠ 0)
        • Reciprocal: (1/g)' = −g'/g²

        Example: (x²·sin x)' = 2x·sin x + x²·cos x

        Think of f·g as an area. When both sides grow, the area gains two strips, Δf·g and f·Δg. The small corner Δf·Δg disappears in the limit. That is the two terms of the product rule.

        Why, from the definition

        • Product: add and subtract f(x + h)·g(x) in the numerator:
        • f(x + h)g(x + h) − f(x)g(x)
        • = f(x + h)·(g(x + h) − g(x)) + (f(x + h) − f(x))·g(x)
        • Divide by h and let h → 0. Since f is differentiable it is continuous, so f(x + h) → f(x), and the result is f·g' + f'·g.
        • Reciprocal: (1/g(x + h) − 1/g(x))/h = −(g(x + h) − g(x))/h · 1/(g(x + h)·g(x)) → −g'/g²
        • Quotient: f/g = f·(1/g), so (f/g)' = f'/g − f·g'/g² = (f'·g − f·g')/g²

        Composite functions (chain rule)

        (f(g(x)))' = f'(g(x))·g'(x)

        The derivative of the outer function, at the inner function, times the derivative of the inner function.

        • ((3x² + 1)⁵)' = 5(3x² + 1)⁴·6x
        • (√(x² + 1))' = 2x/(2√(x² + 1)) = x/√(x² + 1)
        • (sin(x³))' = cos(x³)·3x²

        A small change in x is multiplied by g' on the way through g, and that result is multiplied by f' on the way through f. Rates multiply.

        Why, from the definition

        • Write t = g(x + h) and b = g(x). Multiply by 1, as long as t ≠ b:
        • (f(t) − f(b))/h = (f(t) − f(b))/(t − b) · (t − b)/h
        • t → b because g is continuous. The first quotient → f'(g(x)), the second → g'(x).
        • The case g(x + h) = g(x) near x needs a small extra argument in a full proof; the formula stays the same.

        Trigonometric functions

        • (sin x)' = cos x
        • (cos x)' = −sin x
        • (tan x)' = 1/cos²x = 1 + tan²x
        • With an inner function g: (sin g)' = cos g·g'  ·  (cos g)' = −sin g·g'  ·  (tan g)' = g'/cos²g

        The green curve is the slope of the blue one. At the top of sin x the tangent is flat, and cos x = 0 there.

        Why, from the definition

        • Two base limits: lim_(h→0) (sin h)/h = 1 and lim_(h→0) (cos h − 1)/h = 0
        • Sine: use sin(x + h) = sin x·cos h + cos x·sin h (Trig base › Identities):
        • (sin(x + h) − sin x)/h = sin x·(cos h − 1)/h + cos x·(sin h)/h → cos x
        • Cosine: use cos(x + h) = cos x·cos h − sin x·sin h:
        • (cos(x + h) − cos x)/h = cos x·(cos h − 1)/h − sin x·(sin h)/h → −sin x
        • Tangent: the quotient rule on sin x / cos x gives (cos²x + sin²x)/cos²x = 1/cos²x

        Exponential and logarithmic functions

        • (eˣ)' = eˣ
        • (aˣ)' = aˣ·ln a  (a > 0)
        • (ln x)' = 1/x  (x > 0)
        • (log_a x)' = 1/(x·ln a)
        • With an inner function f: (ef)' = ef·f'  ·  (af)' = af·ln a·f'  ·  (ln f)' = f'/f

        eˣ and ln x mirror each other in y = x. eˣ has slope 1 at x = 0, and ln x has slope 1 at x = 1.

        Why, from the definition

        • eˣ: (eˣ⁺ʰ − eˣ)/h = eˣ·(eʰ − 1)/h → eˣ, because lim_(h→0) (eʰ − 1)/h = 1. That is what makes e special: the tangent at 0 has slope 1 (Precalc › Exp & log calculus).
        • aˣ: aˣ = ex·ln a, so the chain rule gives aˣ·ln a.
        • ln x: (ln(x + h) − ln x)/h = ln(1 + h/x)/h. With t = h/x this is (1/x)·ln(1 + t)/t → 1/x, because lim_(t→0) ln(1 + t)/t = 1.
        • log_a x: log_a x = ln x / ln a, a constant times ln x.
        • ln f: the chain rule with outer ln and inner f.

        3Differentiability

        Differentiable at a point

        f differentiable at a  ⇔  lim_(h→0) (f(a + h) − f(a))/h exists and is finite.

        One-sided derivatives:

        • f'₋(a) = lim_(h→0⁻) (f(a + h) − f(a))/h
        • f'₊(a) = lim_(h→0⁺) (f(a + h) − f(a))/h
        • f differentiable at a  ⇔  f'₋(a) = f'₊(a), both finite

        Differentiable on (a, b): at every point. On [a, b]: also one-sided at the ends.

        Differentiable implies continuous

        f differentiable at a  ⇒  f continuous at a

        The other way is false: f(x) = |x| is continuous at 0 and not differentiable there.

        Proof: f(x) − f(a) = (f(x) − f(a))/(x − a) · (x − a) → f'(a)·0 = 0, so f(x) → f(a).

        Use it backwards: not continuous at a ⇒ not differentiable at a. No calculation needed.

        Where a function is not differentiable

        Four ways the limit of the quotient can fail:

        • Not continuous: a jump or a hole. Example: f(x) jumps at a.
        • Corner: f'₋(a) ≠ f'₊(a). Example: |x| at 0.
        • Vertical tangent or cusp: the quotient → ±∞. Examples: ∛x and ∛(x²) at 0.
        • Oscillation: the quotient has no limit. Example: x·sin(1/x) at 0, with f(0) = 0.

        Also, of course: a point outside the domain.

        Limit of the derivative

        f continuous at a, differentiable for x ≠ a near a, and lim_(x→a) f'(x) = L:

        • L finite  ⇒  f differentiable at a and f'(a) = L
        • L = ±∞  ⇒  f not differentiable at a
        • One-sided too: lim_(x→a⁻) f'(x) = f'₋(a) and lim_(x→a⁺) f'(x) = f'₊(a)

        Consequence: if f'(a) exists and lim_(x→a) f'(x) exists, then the two are equal, so f' is continuous at a. A derivative function can't have a hole or a jump.

        Why: by the mean value theorem (Derivatives › 7), (f(x) − f(a))/(x − a) = f'(c) for some c between a and x. As x → a, c → a, so the quotient has the same limit as f'.

        Continuity of f at a is required. If lim_(x→a) f'(x) does not exist, the theorem says nothing: go to the definition. Example: x²·sin(1/x), f(0) = 0, has f'(0) = 0, yet lim_(x→0) f'(x) does not exist.

        When the derivative formula is undefined at a point

        If the formula for f' has a denominator that is 0 at a, or is undefined at a in any other way, treat it as a warning sign. It is not yet a verdict: the formula only holds where the rules were allowed.

        • Usually not differentiable: f = ∛x, f' = 1/(3∛(x²)) → +∞ at 0. Not differentiable.
        • But not always: f = √(x⁴), f' = 4x³/(2√(x⁴)) is undefined at 0, yet f = x² is differentiable there.

        Decide with the limit of the derivative (if f is continuous at a), or with the definition.

        4Arithmetic of differentiability

        Arithmetic of differentiable functions

        f, g differentiable at a  ⇒  these are differentiable at a:

        • f ± g, with (f ± g)'(a) = f'(a) ± g'(a)
        • c·f, with (c·f)'(a) = c·f'(a)
        • f·g, with (f·g)'(a) = f'(a)·g(a) + f(a)·g'(a)
        • f/g, if g(a) ≠ 0, with (f/g)'(a) = (f'(a)·g(a) − f(a)·g'(a))/g(a)²

        Composition: g differentiable at a and f differentiable at g(a)  ⇒  f∘g differentiable at a, with (f∘g)'(a) = f'(g(a))·g'(a)

        Differentiable on their whole domain: polynomials, sin, cos, tan, eˣ, aˣ, ln. Roots: on x > 0, not at 0. |x|: everywhere except 0.

        When you can't use the arithmetic

        The theorem only works in one direction: all parts differentiable ⇒ the result is differentiable. If one part is not differentiable at a, the theorem says nothing. Go to the definition.

        • Sum: f differentiable, g not ⇒ f + g not. If it were, g = (f + g) − f would be differentiable.
        • Both not differentiable, sum is: |x| + (−|x|) = 0
        • Product is differentiable: x·|x| at 0, and |x|·|x| = x²
        • Product is not: 1·|x| at 0
        • Composition: |x|² is differentiable at 0, |sin x| and sin|x| are not.

        The factor x flattens the corner: x·|x| has tangent slope 0 from both sides of 0.

        5Local extrema

        Local extremum point

        • Local maximum at a: ∃δ > 0 : |x − a| < δ ⇒ f(x) ≤ f(a)
        • Local minimum at a: ∃δ > 0 : |x − a| < δ ⇒ f(x) ≥ f(a)

        "Local" means compared only with nearby points. A local maximum can be lower than a local minimum somewhere else.

        Fermat's theorem

        f has a local extremum at an interior point a and f is differentiable at a  ⇒  f'(a) = 0

        The other way is false: f(x) = x³ has f'(0) = 0 and no extremum at 0.

        Proof (maximum): near a, f(x) − f(a) ≤ 0. So the quotient (f(x) − f(a))/(x − a) is ≥ 0 from the left and ≤ 0 from the right. Both one-sided limits equal f'(a), so f'(a) = 0.

        Critical points and the first derivative test

        A critical point is an interior point of the domain where f'(a) = 0 or f'(a) does not exist. Every local extremum is at a critical point, but not every critical point is an extremum.

        First derivative test (f continuous at a):

        • f' changes from + to − at a ⇒ local maximum
        • f' changes from − to + at a ⇒ local minimum
        • No sign change ⇒ no extremum

        The test works because the sign of f' tells where f rises and falls (Derivatives › 7, Monotonicity).

        6Absolute extrema

        Absolute extremum point

        • Absolute maximum of f on D at a ∈ D: ∀x ∈ D : f(x) ≤ f(a)
        • Absolute minimum of f on D at a ∈ D: ∀x ∈ D : f(x) ≥ f(a)
        • The value is unique on a given domain: one largest value, one smallest value.
        • The point need not be: sin x reaches its maximum 1 at infinitely many points.
        • It may not exist.
        • Where to look: a critical point inside the domain, or an end of the domain.

        Extreme value theorem

        f continuous on a closed interval [a, b]  ⇒  ∃c, d ∈ [a, b] : ∀x ∈ [a, b] : f(c) ≤ f(x) ≤ f(d)

        A continuous function on a closed interval reaches an absolute minimum and an absolute maximum.

        Both conditions are needed. Functions with no absolute extremum:

        • Open interval: f(x) = x on (0, 1) has neither.
        • Open at one end: f(x) = 1/x on (0, 1] has no maximum.
        • Not bounded domain: f(x) = eˣ on ℝ has neither.
        • Not continuous: f(x) = x on [0, 1), f(1) = 0, has no maximum.

        Functions that go to infinity at both ends

        f continuous on (a, b) (a, b may be ±∞):

        • lim_(x→a⁺) f(x) = lim_(x→b⁻) f(x) = +∞  ⇒  absolute minimum, no absolute maximum
        • lim_(x→a⁺) f(x) = lim_(x→b⁻) f(x) = −∞  ⇒  absolute maximum, no absolute minimum

        Why: take any point x₀. Near the ends f > f(x₀), so outside some closed interval [c, d] containing x₀ the function is above f(x₀). On [c, d] the extreme value theorem gives a minimum, and it is the minimum everywhere.

        Same idea with a finite limit: if both end limits are finite and some f(x₀) is below both, there is an absolute minimum (above both: an absolute maximum).

        7Rolle's theorem and the mean value theorem

        Rolle's theorem

        If:

        1. f is continuous on [a, b]
        2. f is differentiable on (a, b)
        3. f(a) = f(b)

        then ∃c ∈ (a, b) : f'(c) = 0

        A graph that leaves a height and comes back must turn around somewhere, and at the turn the tangent is horizontal.

        Proof: by the extreme value theorem, f reaches a maximum and a minimum on [a, b]. If both are at the ends, f is constant and any c works. Otherwise one is at an interior point c, and Fermat's theorem gives f'(c) = 0.

        When a condition of Rolle's theorem fails

        Drop any one condition and the conclusion can fail:

        • Not continuous at an end: f(x) = x on [0, 1), f(1) = 0. Then f(0) = f(1), but f' = 1 everywhere inside.
        • Not differentiable inside: f(x) = |x| on [−1, 1]. Then f(−1) = f(1), but f' = ±1.
        • Different end values: f(x) = x on [0, 1]. Then f' = 1 everywhere.

        The conditions are sufficient, not necessary: when one fails, a point with f' = 0 may still exist. The theorem just doesn't promise it.

        Mean value theorem

        f continuous on [a, b] and differentiable on (a, b)  ⇒  ∃c ∈ (a, b) : f'(c) = (f(b) − f(a))/(b − a)

        Somewhere the tangent is parallel to the chord: the instant rate equals the average rate. Rolle's theorem is the case f(a) = f(b).

        Proof: apply Rolle's theorem to h(x) = f(x) − (f(b) − f(a))/(b − a)·(x − a), which has h(a) = h(b) = f(a).

        Monotonicity

        f continuous on an interval I and differentiable inside it:

        • f' > 0 inside I ⇒ f strictly increasing on I
        • f' < 0 inside I ⇒ f strictly decreasing on I
        • f' = 0 inside I ⇒ f constant on I
        • f' = g' inside I ⇒ f = g + c on I
        • Comparing: f(a) ≥ g(a) and f' > g' for x > a ⇒ f(x) > g(x) for x > a

        Why: for x₁ < x₂ the mean value theorem gives f(x₂) − f(x₁) = f'(c)·(x₂ − x₁). The sign of f'(c) decides.

        It must be an interval. 1/x has f' < 0 on its domain but is not decreasing there. The converse is weaker: x³ is strictly increasing with f'(0) = 0. A f' that is 0 only at single points doesn't spoil strict increase.

        Roots of f and roots of f'

        • Between two roots of f there is a root of f' (Rolle).
        • f has n roots ⇒ f' has at least n − 1 roots
        • f' has k roots ⇒ f has at most k + 1 roots
        • f' has no roots ⇒ f has at most one root
        • A polynomial of degree n has at most n real roots; if n is odd, at least one.

        These count at most. To show a root really exists, use the intermediate value theorem (Limits › 8). All of this assumes f is differentiable on the whole interval.

        8Convexity and concavity

        Convex and concave

        The second derivative is the derivative of the derivative: f'' = (f')'.

        f differentiable on an interval I:

        • Convex on I: f' increasing. The graph bends upward, lies above its tangents and below its chords.
        • Concave on I: f' decreasing. The graph bends downward, lies below its tangents and above its chords.

        Test: f'' > 0 on I ⇒ convex  ·  f'' < 0 on I ⇒ concave

        On a convex stretch the slope keeps growing; on a concave stretch it keeps shrinking. That is why the sign of f'', the rate of change of the slope, decides.

        Inflection point

        a is an inflection point of f if f is continuous at a, has a tangent there, and changes from convex to concave (or the other way) at a.

        • Candidates: f''(a) = 0 or f''(a) undefined, with a in the domain.
        • f''(a) = 0 is not enough: x⁴ has f''(0) = 0 and is convex on both sides. The sign of f'' must change.
        • f'' may be undefined at an inflection point: ∛x at 0, with a vertical tangent.
        • A sign change at a point outside the domain is not an inflection point: 1/x at 0.

        At an inflection point the graph crosses its own tangent.

        Second derivative test

        f'(a) = 0 and f''(a) exists:

        • f''(a) > 0 ⇒ local minimum
        • f''(a) < 0 ⇒ local maximum
        • f''(a) = 0 ⇒ no conclusion: x⁴ (minimum), −x⁴ (maximum), x³ (neither)

        A horizontal tangent on a convex stretch can only be a bottom, on a concave stretch only a top.

        Inequalities from convexity

        • Convex on I, a ∈ I: f(x) ≥ f(a) + f'(a)·(x − a) for all x ∈ I
        • Concave on I, a ∈ I: f(x) ≤ f(a) + f'(a)·(x − a) for all x ∈ I
        • Convex on [a, b]: below the chord between a and b. Concave: above it.

        The right side is the tangent line at a. If one side of an inequality is a line, it is often a tangent or a chord of the other side.

        1What an integral is

        Integrals and antiderivatives

        F is an antiderivative of f when F'(x) = f(x). Then

        ∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + C

        Integration is differentiation run backwards: F' = f  ⇔  ∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + C

        In words: differentiating asks "how fast is this function changing?". Integrating asks the reverse: "which function changes like this?". If you know the speed at every moment, the integral gives you back the position.

        Why the + C: a constant has derivative 0, so x², x² + 1 and x² − 7 all have derivative 2x. The derivative can't tell them apart, so the integral gives the whole family: ∫ 2x dx = x² + C, where C is any number.

        Check any answer by differentiating it: you must get the function under the integral back.

        Every antiderivative is the same curve moved up or down. Moving a curve up doesn't change its slope, so all of them have the same derivative.

        The integral sign and its bounds

        • Indefinite integral: ∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + C. No bounds. The answer is a family of functions.
        • Definite integral: ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx. The answer is one number.

        Reading the symbol: ∫ is a stretched S, for "sum". f(x) is the integrand, the function being integrated. a is the lower bound and b the upper bound: where the sum starts and ends on the x-axis. dx says that x is the variable.

        What the number means: the definite integral is the signed area between the graph and the x-axis from a to b. Area above the axis counts plus, area below counts minus (Integrals › 7).

        The two kinds are tied together by Newton–Leibniz (Integrals › 2): find F from the indefinite integral, then ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a).

        What dx means

        dx names the variable you integrate by, and stands for a very thin width along the x-axis.

        The picture: cut the area under the graph into very thin vertical slices. One slice at x is almost a rectangle: height f(x), width dx, area f(x) dx. The integral adds up all these slices from a to b. That is why f(x) and dx are written next to each other, like a multiplication.

        Which variable: in ∫ x·t dt the variable is t, so x is treated as a constant. Change the letter after d and you change the question.

        How thin is "very thin"? Darboux and Riemann sums (next card) make it exact: the slices get thinner and thinner, and the integral is what the sum tends to.

        Darboux and Riemann sums

        Split [a, b] into n strips of width Δx = (b − a)/(n), with points x_(0) = a < x_(1) < … < x_(n) = b.

        • Lower sum L: each strip's rectangle is as high as the lowest value of f on it.
        • Upper sum U: each rectangle is as high as the highest value of f on it.
        • Riemann sum: any point c_(i) in each strip: ∑_(i=1)^(n) f(c_(i))·Δx. It always lies between L and U.
        • f is integrable on [a, b] when L and U come together as the strips get thinner. Then ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx = lim_(n→∞) ∑_(i=1)^(n) f(c_(i))·Δx
        • Theorem: every function continuous on [a, b] is integrable on it.

        In words: we can't measure a curved area directly, but we can measure rectangles. The lower sum is too small, the upper sum is too big, and the true area is squeezed between them. Make the strips thinner and the two estimates close in on the same number: that number is the integral.

        For f(x) = x² on [0, 1]: the green rectangles are the lower sum, the outlined ones the upper sum. With 4, 8, 16 strips the gap between them shrinks towards the exact area 1/3.

        2The fundamental theorem and Newton–Leibniz

        Fundamental theorem of calculus

        f continuous on [a, b] and F(x) = ∫_(a)^(x) f(t) dt  ⇒  F'(x) = f(x) for every x ∈ (a, b)

        The area function is an antiderivative of the function under it. This is the theorem that links integrals and derivatives.

        What F is: the area under f from the fixed start a up to a moving end x. Move x and the area changes, so F is a function of x. The letter inside is t only because x is already taken by the end.

        Why it is true: move the end from x to x + h. The area grows by a thin strip of width h and height about f(x), so F(x + h) − F(x) ≈ f(x)·h. Divide by h: (F(x + h) − F(x))/h ≈ f(x). As h → 0 the strip becomes an exact rectangle, and the left side is the derivative F'(x) (Derivatives › 1). The area grows at the rate f(x).

        The shaded area is F(x). The thin outlined strip is what moving to x + h adds: about f(x)·h.

        Newton–Leibniz theorem

        f continuous on [a, b] and F any antiderivative of f  ⇒  ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)

        Written: ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx = [F(x)]_(a)^(b) = F(b) − F(a)

        Where it comes from: by the fundamental theorem, the area function A(x) = ∫_(a)^(x) f(t) dt is an antiderivative of f. Any other antiderivative differs from it by a constant: F = A + C (Derivatives › 7, Monotonicity). So F(b) − F(a) = A(b) − A(a) = A(b) − 0, which is the whole area.

        In words: the change in a function between two points equals the area under its derivative over that interval: ∫_(a)^(b) f'(x) dx = f(b) − f(a). If f' is a speed, the area under it is the distance covered.

        Signed area: the result is the signed area. It equals the geometric area only where the function under the integral is ≥ 0.

        The + C doesn't matter here: (F(b) + C) − (F(a) + C) = F(b) − F(a), so any antiderivative gives the same answer.

        Left: the area under f'(x) = x from 1 to 3 is 4. Right: f(x) = x²/2 rises from 1/2 to 9/2, also by 4.

        3Linear properties

        Sums and constants

        • ∫ (f(x) + g(x)) dx = ∫ f(x) dx + ∫ g(x) dx
        • ∫ k·f(x) dx = k·∫ f(x) dx  (k a constant)
        • The same with bounds: ∫_(a)^(b) (α·f(x) + β·g(x)) dx = α∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx + β∫_(a)^(b) g(x) dx

        Why: the derivative has the same rules: (F + G)' = F' + G' and (k·F)' = k·F'. Run them backwards and you get these.

        Use: split a long integrand into simple pieces and integrate each one: ∫ (3x² − 4x + 5) dx = 3·∫ x² dx − 4·∫ x dx + ∫ 5 dx = x³ − 2x² + 5x + C

        There is no such rule for products or quotients: ∫ f·g dx is not ∫ f dx·∫ g dx. Products need substitution or parts (Integrals › 5, 6).

        Properties of the definite integral

        • Same bounds: ∫_(a)^(a) f(x) dx = 0
        • Swapping the bounds flips the sign: ∫_(b)^(a) f(x) dx = −∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx
        • Splitting the interval: ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx = ∫_(a)^(c) f(x) dx + ∫_(c)^(b) f(x) dx

        In words: no width, no area. Walking the interval backwards counts every slice with the opposite sign. And an area can be cut into two pieces at any point c and added back together.

        Splitting is what lets you integrate a piecewise function or an absolute value: cut at each breakpoint (Integrals › 7).

        4Basic integration techniques

        Polynomials and roots

        • Power rule: ∫ xⁿ dx = xⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1) + C  (n ≠ −1)
        • The missing case: ∫ 1/(x) dx = ln|x| + C
        • Constants: ∫ k dx = k·x + C
        • Roots are powers: √x = x^(1/2), ∛x = x^(1/3), 1/√x = x^(−1/2)

        Why: differentiating lowers the power by one and multiplies by it. Integrating does the opposite: raise the power by one, then divide by the new power. Check: (xⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1))' = xⁿ.

        Why n ≠ −1: for n = −1 the rule would divide by 0. That case is ln|x|. The absolute value is there so it also works for x < 0, where ln x is not defined but (ln|x|)' = 1/x still holds.

        Examples: ∫ √x dx = ∫ x^(1/2) dx = (x^(3/2))/(3/2) + C = (2/3)·x√x + C  ·  ∫ 1/(x³) dx = ∫ x⁻³ dx = (x⁻²)/(−2) + C = −1/(2x²) + C

        A linear function to a power

        • ∫ (ax + b)ⁿ dx = ((ax + b)ⁿ⁺¹)/(a(n + 1)) + C  (n ≠ −1, a ≠ 0)
        • ∫ 1/(ax + b) dx = (1/a)·ln|ax + b| + C

        Why the extra 1/a: differentiating (ax + b)ⁿ⁺¹ gives (n + 1)(ax + b)ⁿ·a. The inside's derivative a appears (chain rule, Derivatives › 2), so we divide by it in advance.

        Example: ∫ (3x − 1)⁴ dx = ((3x − 1)⁵)/(3·5) + C = ((3x − 1)⁵)/(15) + C

        This works only when the inside is linear. For (x² + 1)⁴ the inside's derivative 2x is not a constant: expand it, or use substitution if x is also there (Integrals › 5).

        Integrals that give ln

        ∫ f'(x)/(f(x)) dx = ln|f(x)| + C

        Why: by the chain rule, (ln|f(x)|)' = f'(x)/f(x) (Derivatives › 2).

        How to spot it: the numerator is the derivative of the denominator, maybe up to a constant.

        Examples: ∫ 2x/(x² + 1) dx = ln(x² + 1) + C (no |·| needed: x² + 1 > 0)  ·  ∫ cos x/sin x dx = ln|sin x| + C

        Almost the right numerator? Fix it first: the method "Bring a quotient to the form f′/f".

        Sine and cosine

        • ∫ sin x dx = −cos x + C
        • ∫ cos x dx = sin x + C
        • ∫ sin(ax + b) dx = −(1/a)·cos(ax + b) + C
        • ∫ cos(ax + b) dx = (1/a)·sin(ax + b) + C

        Why: (cos x)' = −sin x, so (−cos x)' = sin x, and (sin x)' = cos x (Derivatives › 2). Watch the minus sign: it belongs to the integral of sin.

        Example: ∫ cos(2x + 1) dx = (1/2)·sin(2x + 1) + C. Check: ((1/2)·sin(2x + 1))' = (1/2)·cos(2x + 1)·2 = cos(2x + 1)

        When the inside is not linear, like sin(x²), these rules don't work. Substitution does, when the inside's derivative is also there: ∫ x·sin(x²) dx (Integrals › 5).

        Exponential functions

        • ∫ eˣ dx = eˣ + C
        • ∫ e^(ax + b) dx = (1/a)·e^(ax + b) + C  (a ≠ 0)

        Why: eˣ is its own derivative (Derivatives › 2). With a linear inside, the chain rule brings out an a, so we divide by it.

        Example: ∫ e^(−2x) dx = −(1/2)·e^(−2x) + C

        5Substitution

        What the substitution does

        ∫ f(g(x))·g'(x) dx = ∫ f(t) dt, with t = g(x) and dt = g'(x) dx

        With bounds: ∫_(a)^(b) f(g(x))·g'(x) dx = ∫_(g(a))^(g(b)) f(t) dt

        Where it comes from: it is the chain rule run backwards. If F' = f, the chain rule gives (F(g(x)))' = f(g(x))·g'(x). So the integral of the right side is F(g(x)) + C = F(t) + C, which is exactly ∫ f(t) dt.

        What dt = g'(x) dx means: a thin slice of width dx on the x-axis becomes a slice of width dt = g'(x)·dx on the t-axis. Where g changes fast, the slices are stretched, and the integrand is rescaled to match, so the area stays the same.

        How to spot it: a function inside another function (the inside is g(x)), and the inside's derivative g'(x) is also multiplying, maybe up to a constant.

        ∫_(0)^(1) 2x·e^(x²) dx with t = x² becomes ∫_(0)^(1) eᵗ dt. The graphs look different, but the areas are the same: e − 1 ≈ 1.72.

        6Integration by parts

        Integration by parts formula

        ∫ u·v' dx = u·v − ∫ u'·v dx

        With bounds: ∫_(a)^(b) u·v' dx = [u·v]_(a)^(b) − ∫_(a)^(b) u'·v dx

        Where it comes from, step by step:

        1. Product rule (Derivatives › 2): (u·v)' = u'·v + u·v'
        2. Integrate both sides: u·v = ∫ u'·v dx + ∫ u·v' dx. The left side is an antiderivative of its own derivative.
        3. Move one integral across: ∫ u·v' dx = u·v − ∫ u'·v dx

        What it buys you: it swaps one integral for another. It helps when the new one, ∫ u'·v dx, is easier: you choose u as the factor that gets simpler when you differentiate it.

        The big rectangle minus the small one is u₂v₂ − u₁v₁. The curve splits that L-shaped region into the area under it, ∫ v du, and the area beside it, ∫ u dv. So ∫ u dv = [uv] − ∫ v du.

        7Definite integrals and area

        Integral value and area

        • Value of the integral: ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx. Parts below the x-axis count negative.
        • Area between the graph and the x-axis: ∫_(a)^(b) |f(x)| dx. Everything counts positive.

        In words: the integral adds heights times widths, and a height below the axis is a negative number. So a piece below the axis subtracts. The area is a geometric size, never negative: you flip the negative pieces before adding.

        When they are equal: only when f ≥ 0 on the whole interval.

        For sin x on [0, 2π] the two humps cancel in the integral but add up in the area. Try other functions live in Precalc › Integrals.

        Area between two curves

        Where f(x) ≥ g(x) on [a, b]:  area = ∫_(a)^(b) (f(x) − g(x)) dx

        In words: each thin slice now goes from the lower curve up to the upper curve, so its height is top − bottom. This works anywhere, even below the x-axis, because the difference is still the slice's height.

        When the curves cross inside the interval, the top one changes. Split at the crossing points and use top − bottom on each piece.

        y = x and y = x³ cross at −1, 0 and 1. On (0, 1) the line is on top, on (−1, 0) the cubic is.

        Even and odd functions on a symmetric interval

        • Even (f(−x) = f(x), mirror image in the y-axis): ∫_(−a)^(a) f(x) dx = 2∫_(0)^(a) f(x) dx
        • Odd (f(−x) = −f(x), symmetric about the origin): ∫_(−a)^(a) f(x) dx = 0, but the area is 2∫_(0)^(a) |f(x)| dx

        Examples: x², cos x, |x| are even. x³, sin x, x are odd.

        Why: an even graph has two equal halves, so do one and double it. An odd graph's left half is the right half flipped below the axis, so their signed values cancel. The areas don't cancel: both halves have the same size.

        8Estimating integrals

        Comparison of integrals

        f(x) ≤ g(x) for all x ∈ [a, b] (a < b)  ⇒  ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx ≤ ∫_(a)^(b) g(x) dx

        In words: if one graph is never above the other, the area under it can't be bigger. Each thin slice of f is no taller than the matching slice of g.

        Use it to estimate an integral you can't calculate: put it between two simpler functions whose integrals you can calculate.

        Bounds from the minimum and maximum

        f continuous on [a, b], with minimum m and maximum M there  ⇒  m(b − a) ≤ ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx ≤ M(b − a)

        Why: compare f with the two constant functions m ≤ f(x) ≤ M. A constant's integral is a rectangle: height times width.

        Finding m and M: check the two ends and the critical points inside (Derivatives › 6). Only when f is monotone on the interval are they simply f(a) and f(b).

        The integral is the area under the curve. It is more than the low rectangle (green) and less than the high one (blue). Here the maximum is inside the interval, not at an end.

        9Improper integrals

        What an improper integral is

        • ∫_(a)^(∞) f(x) dx = lim_(r→∞) ∫_(a)^(r) f(x) dx
        • ∫_(−∞)^(b) f(x) dx = lim_(r→−∞) ∫_(r)^(b) f(x) dx
        • Both bounds infinite: split at any point c: ∫_(−∞)^(∞) f(x) dx = ∫_(−∞)^(c) f(x) dx + ∫_(c)^(∞) f(x) dx. Both pieces must converge.

        It converges if the limit is a finite number, and diverges otherwise (infinite, or no limit).

        In words: we can't plug in ∞, so we stop at a finite end r, calculate as usual, and then let r run off to infinity (limits at infinity: Limits › 1). An area that goes on forever can still add up to a finite number, if the graph falls towards 0 fast enough.

        ∫_(1)^(r) 1/(x²) dx = 1 − 1/r → 1: the endless area under 1/x² from 1 on is exactly 1.

        Improper integrals of unbounded functions

        If f blows up at the end a (continuous on (a, b]):

        ∫_(a)^(b) f(x) dx = lim_(r→a⁺) ∫_(r)^(b) f(x) dx

        The same at the end b with r → b⁻. A blow-up inside the interval: split there.

        In words: the bound is finite, but the graph shoots up to infinity at it, so we can't plug it in. Stop just before it at r, calculate, and let r slide to the bad point. Converges or diverges exactly as before.

        ∫_(r)^(1) 1/(√x) dx = 2 − 2√r → 2: the graph has no top near 0, yet the area is 2.

        Harmonic integral (p-integral)

        • ∫_(1)^(∞) 1/(x^(p)) dx converges when p > 1 and diverges when p ≤ 1
        • Near 0, the other way round: ∫_(0)^(1) 1/(x^(p)) dx converges when p < 1 and diverges when p ≥ 1

        Why, for p ≠ 1: ∫_(1)^(r) x^(−p) dx = (r^(1 − p) − 1)/(1 − p). If p > 1 the power 1 − p is negative, so r^(1 − p) → 0 and the limit is 1/(p − 1). If p < 1 it grows to infinity.

        The harmonic case p = 1 diverges: ∫_(1)^(r) 1/(x) dx = ln r → ∞. It is the border: 1/x falls, but just too slowly.

        Why it matters: these are the reference functions for the comparison test (next card).

        1/x (blue) and 1/x² (green) look alike, but the area under 1/x is infinite and the area under 1/x² is 1.

        Comparison test for improper integrals

        0 ≤ f(x) ≤ g(x) for all x ≥ a:

        • ∫_(a)^(∞) g(x) dx converges  ⇒  ∫_(a)^(∞) f(x) dx converges
        • ∫_(a)^(∞) f(x) dx diverges  ⇒  ∫_(a)^(∞) g(x) dx diverges

        In words: under a finite area, a smaller area is finite too. Above an infinite area, a bigger area is infinite too.

        What tells you nothing: the bigger one diverges, or the smaller one converges. Something below an infinite area can be finite or infinite, and something above a finite area too. If you land there, compare with a different function, in the other direction.

        Both conditions matter: f ≥ 0 (no negative parts) and the inequality on the whole [a, ∞), or at least from some point on.

        The same works for unbounded functions near a bad point, with ∫_(0)^(1) 1/(x^(p)) dx as the reference.
